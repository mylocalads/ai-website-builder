If you are replacing windows or doors anywhere in Miami-Dade County, one acronym decides whether the job passes inspection: **NOA**, short for Notice of Acceptance.

## What the NOA actually is

Miami-Dade County runs its own product approval process, separate from the statewide Florida Product Approval system. A manufacturer submits a specific window or door assembly for testing — large missile impact, cyclic pressure loading, water infiltration — and if it passes, the county issues a Notice of Acceptance for that assembly.

The critical word is *assembly*. An NOA does not approve "impact glass" in the abstract. It approves a particular frame, in particular sizes, with a particular glazing make-up, installed with a particular anchor type at a particular spacing, into a particular substrate. Change any of those and you are outside the approval.

## Why it matters on your quote

The inspector who comes to your property is not judging the window by eye. They are checking the installed unit against the NOA it was permitted under: the label on the frame, the sizes, the anchors, the embedment depth. If the installation does not match the document, it does not pass, and the fix is usually not cosmetic.

This is where quotes diverge in ways that are hard to see. Two proposals can both say "impact windows" and describe genuinely different work — different frame series, different glazing, different anchor schedule, and a meaningful price gap that has nothing to do with anyone being cheaper.

## Three things to ask before you sign

**1. Which NOA number is this quoted under?** A contractor who works in Miami-Dade regularly can answer this without hesitation. The number is public and can be looked up on the county's product control search.

**2. Do the sizes on my openings fall inside the approval?** NOAs specify maximum dimensions. Large sliding doors and wide picture windows are exactly where a product runs out of approved size, and where a substitution gets quietly made.

**3. Who is pulling the permit?** If the answer involves you pulling it as an owner-builder, understand what you are taking on. The permit holder is responsible for the work passing inspection, and unpermitted or failed work follows the property — it surfaces at resale, at refinancing, and at building recertification.

## The part people find out late

Opening protection is also what a wind mitigation inspection looks at, and what your insurer prices off. A window package that is genuinely NOA-approved and correctly documented is what makes that report say what you want it to say. One that was installed without a permit, however good the glass is, has no inspection record behind it.
