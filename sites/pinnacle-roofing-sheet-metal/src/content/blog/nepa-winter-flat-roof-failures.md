When a building owner in Wilkes-Barre or Scranton calls us about a leak in
February, the water is almost never coming through the middle of the roof. It
is coming in at the perimeter, at a curb around a rooftop unit, or at a scupper
that iced over three weeks ago and has been backing water up ever since.

That pattern is not a coincidence, and it is not bad luck.

## What a Northeastern Pennsylvania winter actually does to a roof

Three things happen up here that do not happen in milder climates:

**Snow sits.** On a low-slope roof it does not shed. It stays for weeks,
loading the structure and holding meltwater against every horizontal surface it
touches.

**Freeze-thaw cycles work the seams.** Every day the sun warms the membrane and
every night it refreezes. Materials expand and contract at different rates, and
the places where two different materials meet — membrane to metal, metal to
masonry — take that movement over and over.

**Ice dams form at the cold edges.** Meltwater runs toward the drains and
scuppers, hits the colder perimeter, and refreezes. Now the water has nowhere
to go but sideways and up, under flashings that were detailed to shed water
running downhill.

The middle of a membrane field is a single continuous material sitting flat.
It has very little to fail against. The edge is where everything is joined.

## Which is why sheet metal is half the job

Copings, counterflashings, curb flashings, scuppers and terminations are all
sheet metal work. If they are fabricated to fit the building and installed to
handle water moving in an unexpected direction, the roof survives fifteen
NEPA winters. If they are ordered off a shelf and made to fit on site, they
are where the first leak shows up.

This is why our company is called Pinnacle Roofing & Sheet Metal rather than
just Pinnacle Roofing. We fabricate that work in-house rather than subbing it
out, because on a flat roof in this climate it is not a finishing detail — it
is the part most likely to decide the outcome.

## What to look at before winter

You do not need to be a roofer to catch most of this from a roof hatch:

- **Standing water 48 hours after rain.** Drainage is not working, and whatever
  is ponding will be ice in January.
- **Debris at drains and scuppers.** The cheapest maintenance item there is,
  and the most commonly skipped.
- **Open or lifting seams at the perimeter coping.** Where wind gets in first.
- **Failed sealant at curb flashings.** Especially on units that have been
  serviced — HVAC techs walk on and around those all summer.
- **Rust streaks on metal edge detail.** The metal is telling you it has been
  wet longer than it should have been.

## And why the record matters as much as the repair

There is a second reason to inspect twice a year that has nothing to do with
water. Extended manufacturer warranties generally require that the roof was
maintained. When a claim goes in without an inspection history, an otherwise
valid claim can fail on paperwork alone.

A documented maintenance program does two jobs at once: it catches the edge
details while they are still a sealant repair rather than a section
replacement, and it builds the record that keeps the coverage you paid for
actually enforceable.

If it has been more than a year since anybody was on your roof with a purpose,
that is worth fixing before the snow comes.
