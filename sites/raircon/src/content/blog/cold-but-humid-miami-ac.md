The thermostat says 74. The house is cold enough that you have put a blanket on the sofa. And it still feels damp — clammy sheets, windows that fog at the edges, a smell in the hallway you cannot quite place, and eventually dark staining creeping out around the supply vents.

This is the single most common air conditioning complaint we get in Miami-Dade and Broward, and the instinct is almost always wrong. People assume the unit is too weak. It is usually the opposite.

## Cooling and drying are two different jobs

An air conditioner does two things at once. It lowers the temperature of the air, and it pulls moisture out of it — moisture condenses on the cold evaporator coil and drains away. The second job takes time. The system has to run long enough for a meaningful amount of air to pass over that coil.

An oversized system does the first job extremely well and the second job badly. It blasts the house down to the set point in a few minutes, satisfies the thermostat, and shuts off. Then it sits idle while the humidity creeps back up, kicks on again, and repeats. That is called short-cycling, and the result is a house that is cold and wet.

In a dry climate nobody notices. In South Florida, where outdoor humidity sits high year-round and the load on the equipment never really stops, it is the difference between comfortable and miserable.

## What "too big" actually looks like

A few signals that point at sizing or duct work rather than a failing unit:

- Indoor relative humidity sitting between 55 and 65 percent even while the AC is running normally.
- Very short run cycles — the system reaches temperature and stops within a few minutes.
- Rooms at noticeably different temperatures with no obvious cause.
- Dark staining, mildew smell, or visible growth at the supply registers.
- The problem appearing *after* a system replacement, not before.

That last one catches people out. A homeowner replaces a 20-year-old unit, the house gets noticeably more humid, and the assumption is that the new unit is defective. Frequently the old unit was correctly sized and the new one was not — a contractor rounded up a half-ton or a full ton "for safety," which is a nearly free way to make the sale feel generous and a very expensive way to run a house.

## Duct work is the other half of it

Sizing is only part of the picture. Ducts that are undersized, leaking, poorly routed or simply never balanced will produce the same symptoms even behind a correctly sized system. Air that never reaches the far bedroom is air that never passed over the coil either. Leaks in unconditioned space pull humid attic air directly into the supply stream.

This is why we treat testing and balancing as part of the job rather than an upsell. It means measuring actual air and water flow across the system, comparing it to what the design calls for, correcting what is out of specification, and then verifying the result. It is unglamorous and it is the step most commonly skipped, because it takes time and does not sell equipment.

## Why it is worth fixing properly

Sustained high indoor humidity is not just a comfort problem. It is the precondition for mold, and mold remediation costs multiples of what correcting the underlying system would have. It also shortens the life of everything in the house that does not like moisture — flooring, cabinetry, drywall, electronics.

The cheapest visit and the cheapest outcome are rarely the same thing. A properly sized, properly balanced system runs longer cycles, removes more moisture, holds a more even temperature between rooms, and generally uses less energy doing it, because it is not repeatedly hammering through startup.

## What to ask a contractor

If you are getting quotes, three questions separate a real assessment from a box swap:

1. **How did you arrive at that tonnage?** The answer should reference a load calculation for your specific house — square footage, orientation, insulation, window area, ceiling height. "The same as what is in there now" is not a calculation, and neither is "we always go a size up."
2. **Will you test and balance after installation?** If the answer is vague, assume it will not happen.
3. **Have you looked at the duct work?** New equipment behind bad ducts inherits every problem the ducts had.
