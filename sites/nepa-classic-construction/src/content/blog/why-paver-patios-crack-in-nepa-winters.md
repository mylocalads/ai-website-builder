Every spring we get the same call. A patio installed two or three years ago has gone wavy. A section near the house has dropped. A retaining wall is leaning out at the top. The homeowner wants to know whether the pavers were defective.

They almost never are. The pavers are fine. What failed is the six to ten inches of material nobody sees.

## What freeze-thaw actually does

Frost depth in Northeast Pennsylvania runs past three feet in a normal winter. Water in the ground under your patio freezes, expands roughly nine percent, and lifts everything above it. Then it thaws and everything settles back down — but not evenly, because the water did not distribute evenly to begin with.

Run that cycle a few dozen times a season and any surface sitting on soil that holds water will move. On a poured concrete slab you see it as cracks, because concrete is one rigid piece and has nowhere to go. On pavers you see it as waves, dips and open joints, because each unit moves independently.

That independence is exactly why pavers outperform concrete here — a heaved paver can be lifted and reset, while a cracked slab is a demolition job. But it only works if the base underneath drains and stays stable.

## What an engineered base looks like

For a patio in this region, the sequence is:

1. **Excavate to depth.** Six to eight inches below finished grade for a foot-traffic patio, eight to ten for anything a vehicle drives on. Not four. Not "until it looks about right."
2. **Compact the subgrade.** The native soil gets compacted before a single stone goes in, in lifts, with a plate compactor.
3. **Build the base in lifts.** Crushed aggregate goes down in two- to three-inch layers, each one compacted separately. One thick lift dumped and rolled once is not a base; it is a pile.
4. **Slope it away from the house.** Roughly a quarter-inch of fall per foot. Water that cannot leave will sit, freeze, and lift.
5. **Bed and lay.** A thin, screeded sand bedding layer — not a thick one used to correct a bad base.
6. **Restrain the edges.** Edge restraint spiked into the base is what stops the perimeter from creeping outward, and the perimeter is always where failure starts.
7. **Sand and compact the joints.** Polymeric sand, swept, watered and set.

None of this is exotic. It is just slower and more expensive than skipping steps, which is why it gets skipped.

## Retaining walls have one extra rule

Walls fail for a related but distinct reason: hydrostatic pressure. Water collects in the soil behind the wall, freezes, and pushes. A wall built without drainage is a dam, and dams need engineering.

Every wall we build gets clean gravel backfill and a perforated drain pipe daylighted to somewhere the water can actually go. In Pennsylvania, walls over four feet typically require engineering and permits — we handle both, and it appears as a line item in the estimate rather than a surprise partway through.

## How to check work you already have

- **Look at the edges.** Are the perimeter pavers spreading, tilting or separating? That is edge restraint failing, and it is the earliest warning.
- **Check the slope.** After a rain, is water standing anywhere on the surface? It should be gone.
- **Look behind a retaining wall.** Is there any visible drainage outlet at the base? If not, there is probably no drain pipe behind it either.
- **Watch the joints.** Wide-open joints with weeds mean the polymeric sand was never properly set, and water is now getting to the bedding layer.

Most of these are fixable before they become a rebuild. A patio that has dipped in one area can often be lifted, the base corrected, and the same pavers reset — which is the whole argument for pavers over concrete in this climate.

## What it costs

Most paver patios in NEPA run **$20 to $45 per square foot installed**, depending on materials, site access, and how much excavation the grade demands. A typical install takes three to seven days; larger projects with retaining walls or built-in features run two to three weeks.

If a quote comes in far under that range, the honest question to ask is which of the seven steps above is not in it.
