Every February we get the same call. There is a brown ring on a bedroom ceiling, or a damp patch where the wall meets the ceiling in an upstairs corner. The homeowner has been outside and looked at the roof from the driveway, and the roof looks perfectly fine. No missing shingles, nothing flapping, nothing obviously wrong.

The roof usually *is* fine. The water did not come through it. It came in underneath it, and it got there because of an ice dam.

## How an ice dam forms

It takes three things, and Northeastern Pennsylvania supplies all of them for about four months a year.

First, snow on the roof. Second, warm air reaching the underside of the roof deck — from a poorly insulated attic floor, from ductwork or recessed lights leaking heat upward, or simply from an attic that cannot vent what gets into it. Third, an eave that stays cold, because it overhangs the outside air rather than the heated part of the house.

Put those together and the snow in the middle of the roof melts while the eave stays below freezing. Meltwater runs down under the snowpack, reaches the cold overhang, and freezes there. Do that for a few days and you build a ridge of ice at the edge of the roof. Every subsequent thaw now has nowhere to go: the water pools behind the dam, works sideways and upward under the bottom courses of shingle, and eventually finds a nail hole, a seam, or the top edge of the drip edge flashing.

From there it is inside the building. It runs along the top of the ceiling drywall until it finds somewhere to soak in, which is often several feet from where it actually entered.

That last point is the one that matters most, and it is the reason so many ice-dam repairs fail.

## Why patching where the stain is does not work

The stain marks where the water stopped, not where it got in. A repair aimed at the stain is aimed at the wrong part of the roof.

Worse, the real entry point — the bottom two or three courses at a cold eave — often looks completely undamaged in July when a contractor is standing on it in the sun. Nothing is torn. Nothing is missing. So the roof gets declared sound, the ceiling gets repainted, and the following February the ring comes back in exactly the same place.

## What actually fixes it

Three things, and they are not all on the roof.

**Eave detailing.** When we tear off, the bottom of the roof gets a proper ice-and-water membrane run up from the eave far enough to sit inside the warm wall line, so that even when water does back up behind a dam, it is sitting on a sealed surface rather than on a lapped one. Drip edge and gutter apron have to integrate correctly with that, in the right order — this is a place where getting the sequence wrong quietly undoes the whole detail.

**Ventilation.** If the deck is warm, you will keep getting dams no matter what membrane is under the shingles. Intake at the soffit and exhaust at the ridge, balanced, so the underside of the deck stays close to the outside temperature. A lot of the older housing stock in Scranton and Wilkes-Barre has adequate 1920s ventilation under modern insulation, which is to say not enough. Correcting it is part of the roof job, not an upsell after it.

**Flashing that was replaced, not reused.** Reused flashing under a new roof is one of the most common things we find, and valleys and wall junctions near an eave are where backed-up water goes looking for a way in.

## What about gutters, and what about heat cable?

Gutters do not cause ice dams. They can freeze solid *because* of one, and a gutter packed with leaves will hold standing water at the eave that makes the freeze start sooner — which is a real reason to keep them clear and correctly pitched. But no gutter product prevents an ice dam, and anyone selling one on that basis is selling you something.

Heat cable is a genuine tool in a genuinely difficult spot — a north-facing valley that never sees sun, say. It is not a fix. It is a way of managing a symptom while consuming electricity every winter, and if it is being proposed instead of addressing insulation and ventilation, ask why.

## If you have a stain right now

Take a photograph of it, note the date, and check whether it grows during a thaw rather than during rain. That distinction alone tells you a great deal — rain-driven leaks track storms, ice-dam leaks track temperature swings.
