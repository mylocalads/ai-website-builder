We get the same phone call several times a year. Someone is pulling up carpet in a house they just bought in Nanticoke, or Plymouth, or the North End of Wilkes-Barre, and there is wood underneath. They want to know whether it is worth anything.

Usually it is.

## Why so many homes here have it

The housing stock in the Wyoming Valley is old. A lot of it went up between roughly 1890 and 1940, when this was coal country and the homes were built quickly but built out of what was available — which meant Southern Yellow Pine, often called heart pine, and later red oak. Wall-to-wall carpet arrived in the sixties and seventies, and a great deal of that original flooring simply got covered rather than removed.

That is good news for you. Wood that has spent fifty years under carpet and padding is usually in far better condition than wood that spent fifty years being walked on.

## How to check before you call anyone

Pick a closet or a corner behind a door. Pull up a small section of carpet and padding — you are looking for three things:

- **Solid planks, not sheet material.** Plank widths, seams every few feet, and a visible grain. If you see a repeating printed pattern, it is engineered or laminate.
- **How much wood is above the tongue.** Look at the edge where the boards meet a doorway or a floor vent. If there is a quarter inch or more above the groove, there is room to sand.
- **Dark staining or cupping.** Water damage near radiators, exterior doors and bathrooms is the most common thing that limits what we can do.

If you find tack strip holes and staples, do not panic. Those are normal and they disappear in the sanding.

## What refinishing actually involves

The process we use on every floor:

1. **Sanding through three progressively finer grits.** Not one pass. Three, because each one removes the scratch pattern the last one left.
2. **Hand scraping** the edges, corners and anywhere a machine cannot reach properly.
3. **A water-pop.** We dampen the raw wood to raise and open the grain so stain penetrates evenly instead of blotching. This step is skipped constantly and it is the difference between an even floor and a patchy one.
4. **An oil-based stain**, in the color you picked off samples we cut on your own wood — not off a chart.
5. **Three to four coats of a high-quality water-based top coat**, in the sheen you chose. Oil-based is available if you prefer the amber tone it gives.

Light foot traffic is usually fine within about 24 hours. Furniture goes back after a few days.

## Heart pine is its own thing

Southern Yellow Pine behaves differently from oak. It is softer, it takes stain unevenly if you skip the water-pop, and it ambers considerably with age — which is precisely why people love it. If you have heart pine, tell whoever you hire, and ask them what they do differently for it. If they say "nothing," keep calling.

## Is it worth it?

Refinishing an existing floor is almost always less expensive than replacing it, and the wood in these houses is not something you can buy new. Old-growth heart pine is not being milled anymore in any quantity, and the tight grain in a 1910 floor came from trees that took two hundred years to grow.

If you have found something under your carpet and you are not sure what it is, take a photo and [send it to us](/contact). We will tell you what you have got and whether it can be saved — that part does not cost anything.
