Nobody hires a contractor because they are excited about footing depth. People hire us because they want to sit outside in July. But the reason a deck around here is still solid in fifteen years has almost nothing to do with the part you can see.

## Frost is the whole problem

Northeastern Pennsylvania frost depth runs deep enough that a footing set shallow will heave. Not dramatically, and not the first winter — but over several cycles of the ground freezing, expanding, thawing and settling, a shallow post moves. Then the deck racks, the ledger works against the house, and the railing you could lean on last year has a wobble in it.

Footings go below the frost line. There is no material choice, no fastener and no railing system that compensates for getting this wrong.

## The ledger is where water gets in

The ledger board is what attaches the deck to your house, and it is the single most common failure point on decks built quickly. Two things have to be right:

- **Flashing.** Water running down your siding has to be directed out and over the ledger, not behind it. A ledger installed without proper flashing rots the rim joist of your house — which is a structural repair, not a deck repair.
- **Attachment.** Through-bolts or structural screws into solid framing, in the right pattern. Not deck screws into sheathing.

If you look at an existing deck and see a board bolted flat to the siding with no flashing visible above it, that is the one to worry about.

## Drainage, not just slope

Water has to leave. That means gapping the decking properly, keeping debris out of the joist bays, and — for anything covered or built low to the ground — thinking about where the water actually goes once it is off the deck. Low decks over poorly draining ground stay wet, and wet framing in a climate with a hundred freeze-thaw cycles a year does not last.

## Then, materials

Once the structure is right, the decking choice is genuinely about how you want to spend your weekends:

- **Pressure-treated** costs less up front, can be stained any color you want, and wants maintenance every couple of years.
- **Composite** costs more up front and gives you those weekends back. It also handles our winters well, though it will still get slick under snowmelt.

Both are fine choices. Neither one saves a deck that was framed badly.
