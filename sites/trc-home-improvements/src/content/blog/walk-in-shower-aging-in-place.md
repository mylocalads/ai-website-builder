We do a lot of this work around Indian Land and Sun City, and the request nearly always arrives the same way: someone has had a scare, or is planning ahead after watching a parent struggle, and they want the bathroom to stop being the most dangerous room in the house.

The good news is that nearly every accessibility decision also happens to make a shower nicer to use. The catch is that most of them have to be made before framing closes up.

## Get rid of the step-over

A tub edge is the actual hazard. Climbing over a fourteen-inch wall of wet acrylic, on a wet floor, is where people fall.

A low-entry pan drops that to an inch or two. A curbless pan removes it entirely, but it needs the floor structure to allow the drain and slope — which is exactly the kind of thing to establish at the consultation rather than mid-demo.

## Grab bars need blocking, not anchors

This is the one that matters most and gets skipped most.

A grab bar screwed into drywall with toggle anchors will hold a towel. It will not reliably hold a falling adult, which is the entire point of it. Proper installation means plywood blocking fixed between the studs, put in while the wall is open, so the bar is anchored into structure.

If the walls are already closed, you are choosing between opening them again or accepting a bar you cannot trust. Decide before demo.

And they no longer have to look institutional — matte black, brushed nickel and brass finishes are all readily available, and they read as fixtures rather than medical equipment.

## Sit down and check the reach

A built-in bench is the difference between a shower someone can use independently and one they need help with. Tile it into the wall rather than adding a folding seat later, and it becomes part of the design instead of an accessory.

Then place the fixtures for someone sitting on it. A handheld on a slide bar you can reach seated, and a valve you can operate from the bench rather than only from the doorway. This is a five-minute conversation at design stage that changes the entire experience of the room.

## The floor is the last thing people think about

Big glossy tile on a shower pan looks superb and is exactly the wrong choice underfoot when wet. Small-format mosaic gives you far more grout line per square foot, and grout lines are grip. A pebble or hex pan is both the safer option and, in most bathrooms, the better-looking one.

## Do it now, not later

The reason to build this into a remodel you were doing anyway is simple economics. Blocking, a curbless pan and a tiled bench cost very little when the walls are already open. Retrofitting them into a finished bathroom means tearing out work you just paid for.

If accessibility is anywhere on your list — even five or ten years out — say so at the consultation. It costs almost nothing to build in and a great deal to add on.
