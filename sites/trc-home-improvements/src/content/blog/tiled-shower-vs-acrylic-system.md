This is the first real decision in almost every bathroom project we quote, and it gets decided badly more often than any other. People pick tile because it photographs well, or acrylic because it is cheaper, and neither of those is the actual question.

## What a custom tiled shower buys you

Design freedom, mostly. A tiled shower can be any size, any shape, any slope. It can wrap a corner, run to the ceiling, take a bench along one wall and a niche exactly where your shampoo bottles actually go. Nothing about it is constrained to the sizes a factory decided to mold.

It also holds value differently. Large-format porcelain, a mosaic pan and frameless glass read as a deliberate choice to anyone walking through the house — including a buyer.

The cost is time and dependency on workmanship. A tiled shower is built in stages: substrate, waterproof membrane, sloped pan, tile, grout, seal. Each of those has to cure or set before the next one starts. And every one of those stages is a place where a contractor in a hurry can cut something you will not be able to see until it fails.

## What an acrylic or composite system buys you

Speed and predictability. The walls arrive as engineered panels, they go up over a properly prepped substrate, and the whole conversion is usually finished in about two days. There is no tile layout to get wrong and no grout to seal.

Maintenance is genuinely easier. A non-porous surface has nowhere for soap scum and mildew to take hold, so cleaning is a wipe rather than a scrub and a grout brush.

The trade-off is that you are choosing from a catalog. Sizes, patterns and finishes are what the manufacturer makes. If your space is unusual, or you want something specific, tile is the only way to get there.

## How to actually choose

Ask yourself three questions in this order:

**Is this bathroom the feature, or the utility?** A master bath you will look at every day for twenty years justifies tile. A rental turnover, a guest bath or a hall bathroom that just needs to work usually does not.

**How long can the room be out of service?** If it is the only full bath in the house, two days versus two weeks is not a preference, it is the whole decision.

**Is the space standard or awkward?** A sloped ceiling, an odd corner, a curbless entry or a footprint the factory sizes do not fit — that is tile territory by default.

## The one thing that matters either way

Whatever goes on the wall, the waterproofing behind it decides whether the shower lasts. Membrane, slope and substrate prep are not optional on either system, and they are the least visible part of the invoice. If a bid comes in dramatically under everyone else's, that gap came from somewhere — and this is almost always where.

I have personally torn out and rebuilt dozens of showers that failed for exactly that reason. Tile or acrylic, the part you cannot see is the part you are actually paying for.
