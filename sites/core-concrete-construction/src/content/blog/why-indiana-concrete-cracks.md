Every concrete driveway in Indiana will crack. That is not a sales line to lower your expectations — it is how the material behaves. Concrete shrinks as it cures, expands in heat, contracts in cold, and moves with whatever the ground underneath it is doing. The question is never whether it will crack. It is where, and whether the crack means anything.

## Freeze-thaw is the reason Indiana is hard on concrete

Central Indiana goes through dozens of freeze-thaw cycles between November and March. Water soaks into the surface of an unsealed slab during the day, freezes overnight, and expands as it does. That expansion has nowhere to go except outward, so it pushes the top layer of the concrete apart from the inside. Repeat that thirty or forty times a winter and the surface starts flaking away in sheets. That is spalling, and it is the single most common way concrete fails here.

De-icing salt makes it worse. Salt drives more water into the surface and increases the number of freeze cycles the slab goes through, which is why the concrete right where you park in winter usually looks worse than the rest of the drive.

## Control joints are cracks you paid for on purpose

Those straight lines cut into your driveway are not decoration. A control joint is a deliberate weak line in the slab. When the concrete moves — and it will — it cracks along the thinnest point, which is the joint. You get a straight line in a place that was chosen rather than a jagged one across the middle of your driveway.

Joints have to be cut deep enough, roughly a quarter of the slab thickness, and spaced correctly for the thickness of the pour. Joints that are too shallow or too far apart do not control anything, and the slab cracks wherever it likes. If you are looking at a driveway with random cracking between widely spaced joints, that is usually what happened.

## The cracks that actually matter

Broadly, there are three kinds worth telling apart.

**Hairline surface cracks** are shrinkage from curing. They are cosmetic. Sealing keeps water out of them and they generally go no further.

**Cracks that follow a joint** are the joint doing its job. Nothing is wrong.

**Wide cracks with a height difference between the two sides** are the ones to worry about. If one side sits higher than the other, the ground under the slab has moved — washed-out fill, poor original compaction, or water being sent under the driveway instead of away from it. The concrete is a symptom. Pouring a new slab on the same base gives you the same crack again in a few years.

## What actually makes a slab last

Three things, in order of importance.

**The base.** Excavated, filled where needed, and compacted before anything is poured. A slab bridging a soft spot will crack no matter how good the finish looks on the day.

**Drainage.** Downspouts discharging away from the slab, and a grade that carries water off the concrete rather than under it. Most settling we get called out to is a water problem wearing a concrete costume.

**Sealing.** A penetrating sealer keeps water and salt out of the surface. It is the cheapest thing on this list by a wide margin and it is the one most people skip.

## When repair beats replacement

If the concrete itself is sound and only the ground has moved, leveling the slab back into place is usually the better spend. If the surface is spalling or the slab is cracked through in several directions, leveling just gives you level broken concrete, and replacement is the honest answer.
