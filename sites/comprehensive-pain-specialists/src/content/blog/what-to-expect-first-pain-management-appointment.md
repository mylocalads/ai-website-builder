Most people arrive at a first pain management appointment carrying two things: a long history of pain, and a reasonable amount of dread. The dread usually comes from experience — a string of appointments that ended with "the imaging looks fine," or a fifteen-minute visit that produced a prescription and a follow-up six weeks out.

This is what a first visit at our office actually involves.

## Before you come in

Fill out the new patient forms ahead of time if you can. It sounds like a small thing, but a first appointment spent on paperwork is a first appointment not spent on your pain.

Bring four things:

- **Your medical history**, including any prior diagnoses related to the pain.
- **A current medication list** — everything, including over-the-counter drugs and supplements.
- **Any relevant imaging or test results** you already have. X-rays, MRIs, CT scans, nerve conduction studies. You do not need new imaging to be seen; bring what exists.
- **Your insurance card**, and ideally the answer to one question you asked your insurer beforehand: is this practice in network under your specific plan, and does pain management require a referral or prior authorization?

## The consultation itself

The first appointment is diagnostic. The goal is to identify what is actually generating the pain — a disc, a facet joint, the sacroiliac joint, a nerve root, or something outside the spine entirely — because everything after that follows from the answer.

Expect a full history and a physical exam. Expect to be asked not just where it hurts and how much, but what the pain stops you from doing. Whether you can drive. Whether you sleep. Whether you can lift a grandchild. That is not small talk; it is how treatment gets measured, and it is the question chronic pain patients most often report never being asked.

## Why "the scan looks normal" is not a diagnosis

A significant share of chronic pain does not show up on routine imaging. Complex regional pain syndrome, fibromyalgia, myofascial pain syndrome, and many nerve pain conditions are diagnosed clinically — from the pattern of symptoms, the exam, and the history. A normal MRI rules some things out. It does not rule out pain.

If you have been told nothing is wrong, that is a reason to be evaluated by a specialist, not a reason to stop.

## What treatment can look like

Pain management is not a synonym for pain medication. Our practice performs roughly forty interventional procedures in-house, including:

- Epidural steroid injections and facet joint injections
- Medial branch blocks and radiofrequency ablation
- Sacroiliac joint injections
- Spinal cord stimulation and peripheral nerve stimulation
- Vertiflex, Intracept, and the MILD procedure for spinal stenosis
- Trigger point injections, prolotherapy, and nerve blocks
- Botox therapy for chronic migraine

Which of those is appropriate depends entirely on the diagnosis. That is the whole reason the first visit is spent on diagnosis.

## You leave with a plan

By the end of the appointment you should have a specific diagnosis, a written plan, and a clear picture of what any recommended procedure involves before you agree to it. If a procedure is scheduled, you will be told what it is, what it is meant to accomplish, and what recovery looks like.
