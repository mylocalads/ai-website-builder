Most facility managers can point at the section of floor that gets repaired every year. It is almost always the same geography: the dock-to-pick travel path, the rack aisle turns, the spot where every loaded forklift crosses a construction joint.

That repetition is not bad luck, and it is usually not bad workmanship either. It is two mechanisms compounding.

## Joint damage is self-accelerating

A construction joint transfers load across a gap. As long as the two edges — the arrises — are intact, the wheel rolls across and the load hands off cleanly. Once one edge chips, the wheel no longer rolls across the joint; it drops into it and strikes the far edge.

Every subsequent pass enlarges the defect. A chip becomes a spall, the spall widens the gap, the gap increases the impact, and the rate of damage climbs. This is why a joint that looked fine at the last inspection can be a genuine trip and equipment hazard six months later. It is also why deferring joint work is the most expensive form of thrift available to a facility: the repair scope grows on its own schedule, not yours.

There is a second cost that rarely lands in the floor budget. Industry estimates put roughly a fifth of forklift maintenance spend on damage caused by bad concrete and failed expansion joints. The floor is billing the fleet, and nobody reconciles the two line items.

## Rigid repair material fights the slab

The default fix is a cementitious repair mortar. It is cheap, it is available, and it is rigid.

A concrete slab is not static. It expands and contracts with temperature, it deflects under load, and in a Northeast facility it does both hard — dock doors open onto winter, the slab cycles, and the panels move against each other. A rigid patch bonded into that environment has nowhere to put the movement. It cracks, or it unseats at the bond line, and you are back to the original defect with a fresh seam around it.

There is a second failure mode with rigid mortar in a live building: cure time. If the material has not reached strength before the aisle reopens — and in a working distribution center it frequently has not — the repair is being loaded before it can carry the load. That failure gets blamed on the material. It was the schedule.

## What actually stops the cycle

Three things, in order.

**Diagnose the real failure mode.** Joint-edge spalling, surface delamination, full-depth section loss and a subgrade void under a rocking slab all present similarly from standing height and need completely different repairs. Stabilizing a panel that is rocking on a void is a different job from rebuilding the arris above it, and doing the second without the first guarantees a return visit.

**Use a system that moves with the slab.** Polymer-modified, fiberglass-reinforced repair systems flex with expansion, contraction and deflection instead of resisting them until something gives. That single property is the difference between a repair that survives the next winter and one that becomes next year's line item. It is the reason Fibrecrete-G is our flagship system.

**Match the cure to the operating reality.** If the aisle has to reopen at shift change, the repair has to be back under traffic before shift change. A one-hour cure is not a marketing number; it is what makes it possible to repair a joint properly instead of choosing the fastest thing that fits the window.

## When rigid repair is still right

Flexible systems are not the answer to everything. Where a slab, pavement or structural element has genuinely lost section and needs rigid, load-bearing restoration, traditional concrete repair is correct and we specify it. The mistake is not using cementitious repair. The mistake is using it by default, on a joint, in a moving slab, in a building that cannot give it the cure time it needs.

If the same aisle is on your maintenance schedule every year, the repair is not failing because it was done badly. It is failing because it was the wrong repair for what is actually wrong.
