A coated floor that peels, blisters or delaminates inside a year almost never has a product problem. Industry estimates put over 80% of coating failures on improper substrate preparation, and the pattern is consistent enough that you can usually diagnose it from a photograph.

## The three things that go wrong

**No mechanical profile.** A coating bonds to concrete by keying into an opened surface. If the slab was acid-etched, lightly swept, or simply cleaned rather than mechanically profiled — shot-blasted or vacuum diamond-ground — there is nothing for the resin to hold. The floor looks perfect on handover and lifts in sheets once traffic starts working it.

**Untested moisture.** Concrete moves water vapour. If the slab sits on grade without an intact vapour barrier, or if it was coated before it had dried down, that vapour arrives underneath the coating and pushes. The classic tell is white powder at the edge of a lifted section: efflorescence, which is a moisture signature, not a cleaning problem. No amount of surface prep fixes a vapour drive; it needs a moisture-mitigating system or a different specification.

**Contamination left in place.** Oil, grease, silicones from previous treatments, and curing compounds all sit in the pore structure and block adhesion locally. This produces the maddening failure mode where 90% of the floor is fine and one bay lifts — and that bay is invariably the one under the machine that leaks.

## Why it gets skipped

Preparation is the least visible and most expensive part of a coating job. When coatings are bid purely on price per square foot, prep is the line that gets thinned to win the number — and the customer cannot see the difference until the floor is already down.

That is why we scope profiling, moisture assessment and contamination removal as their own items rather than folding them into an installed rate. It makes the bid look less tidy next to a competitor who has quietly assumed a sweep and a prime. It also makes it possible to compare two quotes honestly.

## Questions worth asking before you buy a floor

- **How will the substrate be profiled, and to what CSP?** "Cleaned" and "etched" are not answers.
- **Will you test slab moisture, and what happens if it fails?** If there is no plan for a failing result, there was no real test.
- **What is the specification for the exposure we actually have?** Forklift abrasion, solvent or acid contact, wash-down, thermal cycling and cold storage are five different problems. Epoxy, polyaspartic, chemical-resistant and slip-resistant systems solve different ones.
- **What is the return-to-service time, and does it fit our window?** A coating loaded before it has cured fails for the same reason a rushed concrete repair does.
- **What happens at the joints?** A coating bridged over a moving joint will crack along it. Joints get treated as joints, not as floor.

## The short version

You are not buying a coating. You are buying a bond between a coating and your slab, and that bond is made almost entirely during preparation. If the quote in front of you does not say clearly how the slab will be prepared and how moisture will be verified, the number is not comparable to one that does — and the floor is a coin flip.
