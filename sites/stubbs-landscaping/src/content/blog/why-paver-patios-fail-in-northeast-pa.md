Every spring we get the same call. The patio has a dip near the door. One corner is riding higher than the rest. The joints have opened up and there is grass coming through. It was fine for the first year or two and now it is not.

Almost none of these are paver problems. The pavers are usually in good condition. What failed is underneath them, and it failed because of decisions made before a single stone was set.

## Freeze-thaw is the thing that finds every shortcut

Northeastern Pennsylvania puts a patio through a genuinely hard cycle. Water gets into the base, freezes, expands, and lifts whatever is above it. Then it thaws and that material settles back down — not always to where it started. Do that repeatedly across a winter, every winter, and any weakness in the build becomes visible.

Up here on Mountain Top it is worse than it is down in the valley, and in the higher ground around Hazleton it is worse still. Elevation buys you more cycles per season. A base that is merely adequate in Wilkes-Barre can be genuinely undersized eight miles away and four hundred feet up.

## The four things that actually go wrong

**The sub-base was too shallow.** Excavation is the least visible and most expensive part of the job, which makes it the first thing a cheap bid trims. You cannot see four inches of missing stone in a finished photo. You can see it after two winters.

**The fill was never compacted in lifts.** Base material has to go in a few inches at a time, with a plate compactor run over each layer before the next one goes down. Dump it all in at once and compact the top and you have a firm surface over loose material. That settles, unevenly, and takes the pavers with it.

**No pitch was built in.** A patio needs a slight, deliberate slope so water leaves rather than sitting in the base. It should be imperceptible when you are standing on it and completely intentional in the build. Water that has nowhere to go is what makes freeze-thaw destructive rather than merely inconvenient.

**Edge restraint was skipped or undersized.** The perimeter of a paver field wants to spread outward. Restraints hold it. Without them the outside course creeps a little more each cycle, the joints open, and once the joints open, water and seed get in and the whole thing accelerates.

## What we do instead

We excavate to depth for the soil we actually find, not to a number that makes the estimate look better. Base goes in and gets compacted in lifts. The pitch is designed and shot with a level, not eyeballed. Restraints are set properly around the whole perimeter. And where a lot has a real drainage problem — and on sloped Mountain Top and Back Mountain lots, many do — we solve that first, because building a patio over standing water is building a patio with an expiry date.

None of this photographs well. It adds days to a schedule and it adds cost to a bid, and it is the single biggest reason two estimates for what sounds like the same patio can be meaningfully far apart.

## What to ask whoever you hire

You do not need to be an expert to sort out a good bid from a bad one. Ask three questions:

- How deep are you excavating, and what are you putting back in?
- Are you compacting in lifts, and with what?
- Where is the water going once this patio exists?

A contractor who builds properly will answer all three without hesitating, because they think about it on every job. If the answers are vague, that is your answer.
