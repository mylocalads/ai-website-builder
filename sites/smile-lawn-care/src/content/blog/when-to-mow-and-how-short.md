Every year around the middle of July we get the same call. The lawn looked good in June, and now there are pale straw-coloured patches spreading across the sunniest part of the yard. The assumption is almost always that it needs more water.

Sometimes it does. More often it is being cut too short.

## Cut height is the whole game

Grass roots grow roughly in proportion to the blade above them. Scalp a lawn down to an inch and a half and you have also shortened the root system that is supposed to be pulling moisture out of the soil in a dry spell. Then the exposed soil surface heats up, dries faster, and the plant has less root to cope with it.

Leave the same lawn at three to three and a half inches through July and two things change. The blade shades its own soil, so less moisture evaporates. And the deeper root system reaches water that the short lawn cannot.

This is why two lawns on the same street, getting the same rain, can look completely different in August.

## The other thing short cutting does

Bare, hot soil is exactly what crabgrass wants. It germinates in warm ground and it cannot establish in a lawn thick enough to shade the surface. So the short cut that browned your lawn in July also hands you a crabgrass problem next spring, which then needs a pre-emergent to fix.

A thick lawn cut high is the cheapest weed control available. Nothing in a sprayer competes with it.

## Sharp blades matter more than people think

A dull mower blade tears the grass rather than cutting it. A torn tip browns off and takes days longer to heal, and it leaves a bigger wound for disease to get into. If your lawn looks slightly grey or whitish for a day or two after it is mown, that is a blade that needs sharpening.

It is the least glamorous part of this work and one of the biggest differences in the result.

## What we actually do through the season

- **Spring:** lower cut to clear winter matting and get air to the crown
- **May–June:** weekly cuts, height coming up as growth speeds up
- **July–August:** highest setting of the year, and we would rather skip a week than scalp a stressed lawn
- **September–October:** back down gradually, and this is the best window of the year to feed
- **Late fall:** final cut a little shorter so leaves and snow do not mat it down

Cutting one third of the blade at a time is the rule worth remembering. Take more than that in a single pass and the plant spends its energy rebuilding leaf instead of root.

## If it has already gone

A browned-out lawn in July is usually dormant, not dead. Dormant turf greens back up when temperatures drop and rain returns in September, and the correct move in August is often to leave it alone rather than to feed it — pushing new growth on a stressed lawn does more harm than good.

Fall is when you fix it: overseed the thin areas, feed it properly, and set the cut height correctly the following spring.
