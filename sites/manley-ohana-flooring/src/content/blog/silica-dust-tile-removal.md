Most people booking a floor removal are thinking about noise and about how many days their house is unusable. Very few are thinking about the dust, and the dust is the part that outlasts the job.

## The number that surprises people

Breaking loose roughly 200 square feet of ceramic tile releases in excess of a hundred pounds of silica dust. That is a modest hallway and a bathroom — not a house. Scale it to the 1,200 square feet of tile that runs through the main living areas of a typical Central Florida home and the figure stops being an abstraction.

Silica dust is not ordinary house dust. The particles are fine enough to stay airborne for hours and small enough to be breathed deep into the lungs, which is why it is regulated as a workplace hazard rather than treated as a mess.

## Where it goes when nobody contains it

On an uncontained job, it does not stay in the room being worked.

It gets pulled into the return vents and distributed through the air handler to every room in the house, including the ones with the doors shut. It settles on top of kitchen cabinets, inside closets, on curtain rails, and in the tracks of every window. It works into upholstery. Homeowners describe finding it weeks later in rooms nobody set foot in — and dealing with it not as one clean-up but as several.

That is the actual cost people miss when they compare two removal quotes. The cheaper number frequently is cheaper because containment was never in it.

## What containment looks like in practice

There is no such thing as a genuinely dust-free demolition, and anyone promising one is selling something. There is a large and measurable difference between contained and uncontained, though, and it comes down to three things:

**Capture at the source.** The equipment runs shrouded, connected to a vacuum, so the dust is pulled into a hose at the point it is generated rather than released into the room and chased afterwards. This is the single biggest factor, and it is a function of what machine is on the trailer.

**Sealing what you are not working on.** Plastic over doorways and openings to areas outside the work zone. It is cheap, it takes twenty minutes, and it is skipped constantly.

**Protecting the HVAC.** If the return in the work area is left open, the system will happily distribute everything you just made. Covering it is not optional.

## What to ask whoever you hire

Ask whether the equipment is vacuum-shrouded or open. Ask whether containment and HVAC protection are included in the quoted price or billed separately. Ask what happens to the debris — whether haul-off is in the number or whether you will be arranging a dumpster.

If the answers are vague, the difference will show up in your house rather than on the invoice.
