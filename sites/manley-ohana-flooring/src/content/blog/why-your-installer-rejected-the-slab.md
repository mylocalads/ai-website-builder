It is one of the more common calls we take. Someone had their tile pulled up cheaply, the room is empty, the new flooring is stacked in the garage, and the installer has walked in, looked at the floor and said he cannot lay over that.

He is usually right.

## Removal and prep are two different jobs

Getting a covering off a slab and getting a slab ready to cover are not the same piece of work, and a lot of cheap removal quotes only include the first one.

When tile comes up, the thinset it was bedded in largely stays. When glue-down carpet or sheet vinyl comes up, a skin of mastic stays. What is left is a slab that is technically bare and functionally unusable — ridged, patchy, and in the case of adhesive, actively hostile to anything you try to bond to it.

## Why it matters more now than it used to

Twenty years ago most of what went back down was more tile, set in more thinset, which is forgiving about what is underneath it. Today most of what goes back down is luxury vinyl plank, and LVP is the opposite of forgiving.

It is thin and it is flexible, so it takes the shape of whatever is under it. A ridge of leftover thinset does not disappear beneath the plank — it shows through as a visible line across the floor, catches the light, and works the joints apart with every footstep until they separate. Manufacturers know this, which is why subfloor flatness and cleanliness are written into their installation requirements and why failures traced to them are excluded from warranty as installation defects rather than product defects.

So an installer refusing to start is not being difficult. He is declining to put his name on a floor that is going to fail and be blamed on him.

## What "ready to cover" actually means

- Old thinset, mortar and adhesive residue ground off, not just knocked down
- High spots taken flat
- No loose material, no fasteners, no tack strip left at the perimeter
- Swept clean, so the condition can actually be inspected
