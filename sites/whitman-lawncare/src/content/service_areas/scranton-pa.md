Scranton lawns sit on old coal-country ground, and a lot of it is fill or shale-derived soil running well below neutral pH. Feeding a lawn here without correcting the acidity first is money spent on nutrients the roots cannot reach — which is why every Scranton property we take on starts with a soil test.

The older neighborhoods bring their own conditions. Mature street trees through Green Ridge and the Hill Section mean heavy shade and root competition, so those lawns need shade-tolerant seed and a lighter feeding schedule than a full-sun property. Newer construction on the outskirts often has thin topsoil over compacted subgrade, where core aeration does more good in one fall than another round of fertilizer would.
