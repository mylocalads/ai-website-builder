If you are planning a remodel in Southwest Florida, one of the first decisions you will make has nothing to do with tile, cabinets or countertops. It is a decision about structure: who is responsible for your project.

There are two common answers, and they lead to very different experiences.

## The traditional split

In the traditional model, you hire a designer or architect first. They produce a plan. You then take that plan out to contractors and collect bids.

On paper this sounds sensible — you get independent design, then competitive pricing. In practice it introduces a gap that you end up standing in.

The designer does not carry construction cost responsibility. The contractor did not draw the plan. When the plan turns out to be more expensive than expected, or something on site does not match the drawing, each one can reasonably point at the other. You are the only person in the room with an interest in resolving it, and you are usually the least equipped to.

That gap shows up as three specific costs:

- **Redesign time.** Plans that were never priced during design come back over budget, and you pay to have them revised.
- **Change orders.** Site conditions that a builder would have flagged during design get discovered during construction, when they are far more expensive to address.
- **Schedule slip.** Every question that has to travel between two companies takes longer to answer than one that does not.

## What design-build changes

Design-build puts the designers and the builders in the same company, under one contract.

That single structural change removes the gap. There is no handoff, because there is nobody to hand off to. The people drawing your plan are accountable for building it, which means cost realism enters the conversation on day one instead of after the drawings are finished.

The practical differences you will notice:

- **One point of accountability.** One team owns every decision and every outcome. There is no finger-pointing, because there is nobody else to point at.
- **Budget feedback in real time.** Designers and builders work side by side from the start, so problems get solved while they are still drawings — which is when solving them is cheap.
- **Fewer surprise change orders.** Site realities are caught during design rather than mid-build.
- **A shorter overall timeline.** Design and pre-construction planning overlap instead of running end to end.

## When traditional still makes sense

Design-build is not automatically the right answer for everyone.

If you have a long-standing relationship with an architect whose work you specifically want, or your project is unusual enough that you want fully independent design before anyone prices it, the traditional route is defensible. Some public and institutional projects also require competitive bidding on a completed design.

For most residential remodels and commercial tenant buildouts, though, the independence you gain is worth less than the coordination you lose.

## Questions worth asking either way

Whichever model you choose, these questions separate a firm that has thought about your project from one that has not:

1. Who is my single point of contact, and who do I call when something goes wrong?
2. When in the process will I get a number I can rely on?
3. How are change orders priced and approved?
4. What is covered by your workmanship warranty, and for how long?
5. How will I see progress — schedules, budgets, daily logs?
6. Are you licensed and insured for work of this type and size?

On that last one, ask for the license number and check it. In Florida, a Certified General Contractor licence can be verified in seconds through the state's DBPR licence lookup.
