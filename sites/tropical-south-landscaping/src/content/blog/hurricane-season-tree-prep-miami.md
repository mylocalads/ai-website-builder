Every year the same thing happens. A system forms in the Atlantic, the cone shifts toward South Florida, and every tree company in Miami-Dade stops answering the phone because they are booked solid for the next ten days.

By then it is too late to do anything useful about the oak leaning over your roof.

## The window is spring, not September

Miami-Dade County's own hurricane guidance tells homeowners to prune ahead of the season rather than during it, and to have the work done by someone qualified. Season runs June 1 through November 30. That puts the useful window in late winter and spring, when crews have availability, the weather is dry enough to get equipment onto a lawn, and the tree has months to respond to the cuts before it is asked to stand up to 90 mph.

Pruning a tree in the 48 hours before landfall does not make it safer. It makes it a tree with fresh wounds and a load of cut debris on the ground.

## What actually fails in a storm

Three things, mostly.

**Deadwood.** Dead limbs are already detached in every way that matters — they just have not fallen yet. Wind finds them first.

**A dense, unthinned canopy.** A canopy that has never been thinned catches wind like a sail. The whole tree loads up, and either large limbs tear out or the root plate lifts. Selective thinning lets air through and drops the load dramatically.

**Limbs grown out over the roof.** These are the expensive ones. The limb does not need to break to cause damage; it only needs to flex far enough to hit tile or fascia repeatedly for six hours.

None of the three are emergencies in April. All three are emergencies in September.

## What proper storm prep is not

It is not hatracking. Topping a tree — cutting the main limbs back to stubs — is the fastest way to make a canopy smaller and the most reliable way to ruin the tree permanently. Miami-Dade County specifically tells homeowners not to allow it.

What grows back after topping is a cluster of weak shoots attached to the outside of the old cut rather than properly joined to the limb. Two or three seasons later you have a tree that is denser than it was, weaker where it counts, and considerably more likely to shed a limb in the next storm than if it had been left alone.

The same applies to palms. Over-pruning a palm into a narrow "hurricane cut" — stripping it back to a handful of upright fronds — does not protect it. The fronds are how the palm feeds itself and how it buffers wind at the crown. Take too many and you get a weaker tree, not a safer one. Dead fronds and seed pods come off; the live crown stays.

## What it should look like instead

Deadwood out. Selective thinning through the canopy so wind passes rather than pushes. End weight reduced on the long limbs, cut back to a lateral that can take over as the new leader. Clearance established over the roof, the driveway and the power drop. And every cut placed at the right point on the limb so the tree can seal it.

That is ISA-standard pruning, and it is what a licensed, insured crew working from a lift should be doing on your property. It takes longer than tipping the ends off, and it is the difference between a tree that survives the season and one that costs you a roof.
