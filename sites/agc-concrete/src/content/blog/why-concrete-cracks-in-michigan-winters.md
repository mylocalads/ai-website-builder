Concrete in Southwest Michigan doesn't fail the way concrete fails in a warmer climate. The problem here isn't heat or UV — it's water that gets into the concrete, freezes, and expands.

## How freeze-thaw damage actually starts

Concrete is porous at a microscopic level. Water works into small surface cracks, pores, or poorly finished areas, and when temperatures drop below freezing, that water expands by roughly nine percent. Over dozens of freeze-thaw cycles each winter, that expansion widens hairline cracks into real ones, and eventually breaks up the surface — a pattern called scaling or spalling.

## What actually prevents it

A few things make the biggest difference in how well a driveway or patio holds up to Michigan winters:

- **Proper base prep.** A well-compacted gravel base drains water away from underneath the slab instead of letting it sit and freeze against it.
- **Control joints cut at the right spacing.** Joints give the concrete a planned place to crack as it naturally contracts and expands, instead of cracking randomly across the surface.
- **Air-entrained concrete mix.** Mixing in microscopic air bubbles gives the water inside the concrete somewhere to expand into, which is standard practice for any exterior concrete poured in a freeze-thaw climate.
- **Sealing.** A quality sealer slows how much water gets into the surface in the first place, which is the difference between a driveway that looks the same in ten years and one that's scaling by year five.

## What to do if you're already seeing cracks

Not every crack means the whole driveway needs to be replaced. Hairline cracks from normal shrinkage are common and often cosmetic. Cracks with height differences between the two sides, cracks that keep spreading, or a slab that's visibly settled are signs it's worth having someone look at it before it gets worse — and before winter does the rest of the damage for you.
