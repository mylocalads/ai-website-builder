The single most common complaint homeowners have about this trade is not price. It is
being told the whole system has to go when it did not have to go.

It happens because the incentives point that way. A capacitor is a small invoice. A
diagnostic visit is a small invoice. A full system replacement is a large one, and a
technician working on commission has an obvious reason to arrive at that answer. You
are then asked to make a several-thousand-dollar decision on the spot, in your own
basement, usually on the coldest or hottest day of the year, with no way to check the
claim.

Here is how to check it anyway.

## Ask what specifically failed

A legitimate diagnosis names a part and a symptom. "The inducer motor has seized."
"The heat exchanger is cracked here, and this is the CO reading." "The compressor is
drawing locked-rotor amps." Those are testable statements, and a technician who has
actually done the work can point at the thing.

A replacement pitch usually skips that step. It talks about the age of the system, the
efficiency of new equipment, and what you are spending on fuel. All of that may be true
and none of it is a diagnosis. If nobody can tell you which component failed, nobody has
found a failed component.

## Age alone is not a diagnosis

Fifteen years is often quoted as the point at which a furnace or air conditioner is
"due." It is a useful planning number and a poor decision-making one. Plenty of
twenty-year-old equipment in this part of Pennsylvania is running fine because it was
sized correctly and maintained. Plenty of eight-year-old equipment is struggling because
it was oversized for the house and has been short-cycling since the day it went in.

The right question is not how old it is. It is what is wrong with it, what the repair
costs, and how much life that repair realistically buys.

## Watch for the parts markup

One widely shared account from a homeowner involved being quoted $300 per pound for
refrigerant that was available commercially for a little over $30 per pound. That is not
a repair price. That is a repair priced deliberately out of reach so that replacement
becomes the only sensible-looking option.

You do not need to know trade pricing to spot this. You need one competing number. If a
second company quotes the same repair at a fraction of the price, the first quote was not
about the repair.

## Get the estimate in writing before anything starts

Verbal numbers grow. A written scope — what is being done, what parts, what it costs —
is the only version that can be held to at the end. It is also the fastest way to find
out whether a contractor is comfortable being held to anything.

For what it is worth, this is the part our own customers write about most often. One put
it as "final cost was within the estimate range." Another wrote that the estimate was
accurate and there were no additional charges or fees. That should be unremarkable. The
fact that people bother to mention it tells you how often it goes the other way.

## When replacement genuinely is the right call

Sometimes it is, and pretending otherwise does nobody any favours:

- **A cracked heat exchanger.** This is a carbon monoxide risk. The furnace gets
  condemned, not patched.
- **A failed compressor on an older system.** The part and the labour approach the cost
  of a new unit, and the rest of the system is the same age.
- **Obsolete refrigerant.** R-22 systems can still be serviced, but the refrigerant cost
  now makes any significant leak repair uneconomic.
- **Repeated failures across different components.** When three unrelated parts fail in
  two seasons, the system is telling you something.

In each of those cases you should still get the reasoning before you get the price, and
you should still get the sizing calculation for whatever is being proposed to replace it.

## The short version

Ask what failed. Ask what the repair costs. Ask for it in writing. If the answers are
vague, get a second opinion — the diagnosis is worth more than the discount.
