After a round of hail and flooding moved through the Miami Valley in spring 2025, a homeowner in Jamestown had three contractors show up at her door uninvited. Not one — three. The hail had been about the size of an ice cube, and the trucks arrived before the puddles had gone down.

The Dayton Better Business Bureau put out a warning about it. Their representative, Sheri Sword, described the pattern in plain terms: some of these storm chasers are in town, and as soon as everything's done, they leave and you can't get in touch with them again. She added the detail that ought to stick with you — reputable contractors don't need to go door-to-door to ask for jobs.

We are a roofing company, so you can reasonably discount our motives here. Read this as an argument to verify everyone, including us.

## Why this is harder in Ohio than you'd think

Most people assume there is a license to look up. There isn't. Ohio does not issue a statewide license for roofing contractors. Some cities and counties require their own registration and pull their own permits, but there is no single state credential you can check to confirm the person on your roof knows what they are doing.

That gap is the entire business model of the truck that showed up after the storm. It is not that they are breaking a rule — it is that in this trade, in this state, there is very little rule to break.

So the burden falls on you. Here is what is actually checkable.

## Four things you can verify in about ten minutes

**A physical address, not just a phone number.** Ask where the office is, then look it up. A company with a building in Montgomery or Greene County has something to lose by doing bad work. A number with an area code from three states away does not. Ours is 3347 Witherspoon Drive in Dayton, and you are welcome to drive past it.

**Manufacturer credentials.** Shingle manufacturers run certification programs, and they take them seriously because their warranty exposure depends on installation quality. CertainTeed's ShingleMaster credential has to be earned and maintained. In a state with no license, a manufacturer credential is one of the few things a third party has actually vouched for.

**A Better Business Bureau file.** Not a logo on a website — the actual file. Search the business name at bbb.org and see the rating, the accreditation date, and whether there are complaints and how they were resolved. Worth noting that the BBB is the organization that issued the storm chaser warning in the first place, which makes an accredited file with them a fairly direct answer to it.

**An estimate in writing.** This is the BBB's own advice: get three written quotes. Anyone who will not put the scope and the price on paper, or who needs a signature before they leave your driveway today, is telling you something important about how the rest of the job will go.

## The insurance wrinkle

If hail or wind did damage your roof, your homeowners policy likely covers the repair minus your deductible, and most policies give you one to two years from the date of the storm to file. That is a real deadline, and it is the pressure storm chasers lean on — the sense that you need to move now.

You do need to get it looked at. You do not need to sign with the first person who offers. Those are different things, and a legitimate contractor will not blur them.

One thing worth watching for: when an insurer is paying, there is a temptation for some contractors to install cheaper material than the claim specified. Ask what is going on your roof, by name, and check that the written estimate says the same thing.

## What a real inspection looks like

Someone should get on the roof. Not look at it from the driveway, not point at a photo of somebody else's hail damage — get up there and photograph yours. Hail bruising shows as granule loss and soft spots you genuinely cannot assess from the ground, which is exactly why the ground-level diagnosis is such a common opening move.

You should end up with pictures of your own roof and an explanation of what they show. If the honest answer is that the roof has several years left in it, you should hear that too.

## And if you already signed

Read the contract for a cancellation window. Many home improvement agreements signed at your door carry a right to cancel within a few days, and if the work has not started, exercising it is usually straightforward. If money has changed hands and the contractor has gone quiet, the BBB and the Ohio Attorney General's office both take complaints.

The storms will keep coming — seventeen confirmed hail reports within twenty-five miles of Dayton in the last twelve months is not an unusual year for Montgomery County. The trucks will keep following them. Ten minutes of checking beats a decade of a roof someone rushed.
