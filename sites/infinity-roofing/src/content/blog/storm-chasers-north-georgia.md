Hail comes through Paulding and Cobb most springs, and the door-knocking starts
within a few days of it. Some of those crews are legitimate. Many are not. After
one North Atlanta hailstorm the Better Business Bureau logged roughly 30% more
roofing complaints than normal, and the phone lines stayed busy for weeks
afterwards.

The difficulty for a homeowner is that everyone at the door says the same
things. Here is what actually separates them.

## The one offer that should end the conversation

If a contractor offers to waive, absorb, cover or "take care of" your
deductible, stop there.

It is illegal in Georgia under HB 423. More to the point, it is not only the
contractor who is exposed — the homeowner is a party to the claim, and a
settlement obtained on a false representation is the homeowner's problem too.
It gets offered because it closes deals with people who do not know the law.

A contractor willing to break that rule in your driveway on day one is telling
you something about the rest of the job.

## Ask where they are, not where they say they work

"We serve all of North Georgia" is not an address. A local contractor has a
fixed place of business you could drive to, and a review history that goes back
further than the most recent storm season.

Reviews are the useful test. Look at the **dates**, not the star rating. A
company working this area for years has reviews spread across years. A storm
chaser's reviews arrive in a cluster, all within a few months of the weather
that brought them here.

## Get it in writing before anyone signs anything

The Georgia Attorney General's consumer alerts on storm fraud all point at the
same pattern: high-pressure demands for a same-day signature, a deposit before
any work begins, and a price that is only good today.

None of those are how legitimate roofing work is sold. A written estimate costs
you nothing, does not require a deposit, and is still valid tomorrow.

## Understand what your policy actually pays

This one is not about the contractor at all, and it catches people out badly.

- **Replacement cost value (RCV)** pays what it costs to replace the roof today,
  minus your deductible.
- **Actual cash value (ACV)** pays the *depreciated* value of the roof.

On an older roof, an ACV settlement can land far below what replacement costs,
and homeowners routinely discover this only when the cheque arrives. It is worth
knowing which one you have before a storm rather than after.

## Why claims get denied

The most common denial reason in Georgia is not fraud or exclusions. It is an
adjuster classifying storm damage as ordinary wear and tear.

That call is easiest to make on a roof with some age on it, or where the damage
is subtle — a bruised shingle looks a lot like a worn one if nobody photographed
it properly. This is the real argument for having a contractor document the
damage *before* the adjuster arrives, and for having someone meet them on the
roof rather than leaving the assessment to a single visit.

A claim decided on a photographic record goes differently from one decided on an
impression.

## A short checklist

Before you sign anything after a storm:

1. Ask for the physical business address, and check it.
2. Read the review dates, not just the rating.
3. Get a written estimate. Pay no deposit for one.
4. Check whether your policy is ACV or RCV.
5. If anyone offers to handle your deductible, end the conversation.
