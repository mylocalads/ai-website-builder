If hail moved through your neighborhood this week, you already know what
happened next. Someone knocked. Then someone else knocked. In the Metroplex it
is not unusual for a homeowner to field half a dozen roofing salespeople across
a single weekend, some of them racing each other up the driveway.

Most homeowners react one of two ways. They sign with whoever seems most
confident, or they decide every roofer is a scammer and do nothing at all. Both
are expensive. Here is the middle path.

## Why they all show up at once

After a significant hail event, crews travel. Sales teams follow storm maps
across state lines, work a market hard for a few months, and move on when the
claims dry up. Inside the industry these operations are called storm chasers,
and the label is descriptive rather than an insult — the business model really
is chasing storms.

That does not automatically make them dishonest. It does mean two things worth
weighing. First, quantity is the point: the model rewards volume of signed
contracts, not depth of work on any single roof. Second, when a workmanship
warranty outlives the company that issued it, the warranty was never worth
anything.

## Four questions that sort it out quickly

You do not need to become a roofing expert. You need four answers.

**"What is your local address?"** Not a service area — a street address you
could drive to. Ask how long the company has operated in this market. A
contractor who has been here through several storm cycles has a reputation
locally that is worth more to them than any single job.

**"Will you be at my adjuster meeting?"** This is the most revealing question
on the list. Being on the roof when the insurance adjuster is there is the
difference between damage that gets documented and damage that gets missed.
A contractor who plans to quote your roof and cash the first check has no
reason to be there, and will tell you it is unnecessary.

**"How long have you been in business relative to your warranty?"** A
twenty-five-year workmanship warranty from a company incorporated last spring
is a piece of paper. Manufacturer-backed warranties — the kind that require
certification such as GAF Master Elite® — survive the contractor, which is
precisely the point of them.

**"Can I see the photos?"** Any inspection worth having produces a photo report
of every slope. If someone tells you your roof is finished but cannot show you
images of the damage, they are asking you to take a stranger's word about
something you cannot see.

## The deductible red flag

At some point in one of these conversations you may hear a version of: *we will
take care of your deductible*, or *we will work with you on that*.

Texas law prohibits a contractor from paying, rebating, waiving, or absorbing
any part of your insurance deductible. The Texas Department of Insurance
publishes guidance on this. It is presented as generosity and it is not — it is
a misrepresentation to your carrier, and you are the one whose name is on the
claim.

The useful way to think about it: if a contractor is willing to misrepresent
this to your insurance company on day one, what else are they comfortable
misrepresenting later?

## Do you actually have damage?

Possibly not. Hail damage depends on stone size, density, the angle they fell,
storm duration, and the age and grade of your existing roof. A five-year-old
architectural shingle roof and a fifteen-year-old three-tab roof in the same
storm are simply not the same case.

Signs worth taking seriously:

- Damage with no discernible pattern across the slope
- Black hail hits where granules have been knocked off
- Dents that feel soft or bruised under thumb pressure
- Impact marks or sharp-edged fractures mid-shingle
- Debris underneath multiple shingles
- Collateral evidence elsewhere — dented gutters, torn window screens, damaged
  AC fins, marks on the fence

That last one matters more than most homeowners realize. Hail does not only hit
roofs, and collateral damage is often the easiest thing to photograph from the
ground.

## The clock is real, even if the pressure is fake

Urgency in a door-to-door pitch is usually manufactured. The deadline underneath
it is not.

Texas homeowners policies generally require prompt notice of a loss, and many
set an outside limit of roughly a year from the date of the storm. The exact
window is set by your policy, so read it or ask your agent. But the practical
risk is broader than any single date: the longer you wait, the easier it is for
a carrier to argue the damage is old, pre-existing, or from a storm you never
reported. Homeowners who were told years ago that damage "wasn't enough to
file" and never followed up are the ones who end up with a roof no insurer will
cover.

## What we would do in your position

Do not sign anything at the door. Not an inspection authorization, not a
contingency agreement, not a "this just lets me look at it" form.

Then get a second opinion from a company with a local address. Ask for the
photo report. If there is damage, file with a documented date of loss and have
your contractor present when the adjuster arrives. If there is no damage, you
have lost an hour and gained a dated record of your roof's condition — which is
worth having the next time someone knocks.
