Your claim is approved. A check arrives. It is thousands of dollars less than
the estimate says the roof costs, and nobody has explained why.

This is the single most common point of confusion in a Texas hail claim, and
it is not a mistake. It is how the policy is designed to work. Understanding it
takes about ten minutes and can be worth several thousand dollars.

## Two numbers, not one

Every claim estimate carries two figures for the same roof.

**Replacement cost value (RCV)** is what it costs to put a new roof on your
house today, at current material and labor prices.

**Actual cash value (ACV)** is RCV minus depreciation for the age and condition
of the roof you had. A fifteen-year-old roof has used up a meaningful share of
its service life, and the policy accounts for that.

Which one your carrier ultimately pays depends on which policy you bought.

## If you have an RCV policy

Most Texas homeowners policies are replacement cost policies, and they pay in
two stages.

The first payment is the ACV amount, minus your deductible. It usually arrives
soon after the adjuster's inspection. This is the check that surprises people,
because it is the depreciated number rather than the full cost of the roof.

The second payment is the depreciation that was held back — the difference
between ACV and RCV. This is called **recoverable depreciation**, and it is
released after the work is completed and certified to the carrier.

Worked through with round numbers: a roof with an RCV of $50,000, $10,000 of
depreciation, and a $5,000 deductible. The first check is $35,000. You complete
the work. The carrier releases the remaining $10,000. Your total out of pocket
is the $5,000 deductible.

## The step most homeowners never complete

That second payment does not arrive automatically. Somebody has to send the
carrier proof that the work was completed to the approved scope.

If your contractor takes the first check, installs the roof, and moves on, the
recoverable depreciation can simply sit there. Homeowners frequently do not
know it exists, because nobody told them the first payment was partial.

When we run a claim, filing that completion paperwork is part of the job rather
than a favor. It is the last step of the process, and skipping it is leaving
your own money with your insurance company.

## If you have an ACV policy

Some policies — increasingly common on older roofs — only ever pay actual cash
value. There is no second check. If your roof is nine years old and the carrier
pays for the remaining life only, the gap between that payment and the real cost
of the roof is yours to cover.

This is worth confirming with your agent *before* a storm rather than after.
The difference between an RCV and an ACV policy on an aging roof can be tens of
thousands of dollars, and the premium difference is usually far smaller than
people assume.

## Where claims fall short, and what to do about it

An approved scope is not always a complete scope. Adjusters cover a great deal
of ground after a major storm and things get missed — a slope not fully walked,
a shingle grade specified below what is actually on the roof, code-required
upgrades left out, collateral damage to gutters or screens never itemized.

The remedy is a **supplement**: a documented request to revise the scope, backed
by photographs and measurements. Supplements are routine and carriers process
them regularly. They also require someone to do the work of assembling the
evidence, which is why a contractor who was present at the adjuster meeting is
in a much stronger position to file one than a contractor who was not.

## Deductibles, plainly

Your deductible is yours to pay. On most Texas policies it is now a percentage
of dwelling coverage rather than a flat amount — 1% has been giving way to 2%,
and in some cases 3%, at renewal. On a $400,000 dwelling, a 2% wind and hail
deductible is $8,000.

Texas law prohibits a contractor from paying, waiving, rebating, or absorbing
any part of it. If someone offers, that is a signal about how they operate
generally, not a discount.

## The short version

- The first check is depreciated and net of your deductible. It is meant to be
  smaller than the estimate.
- On an RCV policy, the held-back depreciation is recoverable — but only after
  the work is certified complete.
- Somebody has to file that completion paperwork. Confirm who.
- If the approved scope looks thin, a supplement is the normal remedy.
- Check whether your policy is RCV or ACV before you need to know.
