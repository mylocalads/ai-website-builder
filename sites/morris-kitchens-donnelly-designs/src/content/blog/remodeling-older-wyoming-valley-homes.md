Most of the homes we work in around Wilkes-Barre, Kingston, Nanticoke, and the
West Side boroughs were built long before anyone assumed a kitchen would have a
dishwasher, an island, or a sightline to the living room. That is not a problem
to apologise for — it is simply the starting condition, and it changes how a
remodel has to be designed.

## The footprint is rarely the constraint

The square footage in these houses is often fine. What is missing is storage and
workflow. A galley with three doorways into it has almost no usable wall,
because every run of cabinetry gets cut short. Closing one of those doorways —
often a doorway nobody actually uses — can hand back six feet of continuous
counter and upper cabinets.

## Where the plumbing actually is

Older homes tend to have a single plumbing stack, and it is usually not where
you would put a sink today. Sometimes it is worth moving; often it is cheaper
and less disruptive to design the room around it and spend the money on
cabinetry instead. This is the kind of call that gets made properly only when
the person drawing the layout has stood in the room.

## Chimneys, radiators, and the things in the way

Disused chimney breasts, radiator runs, and low soffits eat into wall space in
ways that a catalog layout never anticipates. Every one of them is workable —
custom fillers, a bank of drawers at a shallower depth, a run of open shelving
where a cabinet will not fit — but each has to be measured and drawn, not
assumed.

## Why the measuring matters more here than in a new build

In a house built in the last twenty years the walls are close to square and the
floors are close to level. In a 1920s home neither of those is true, and a
cabinet order laid out against nominal dimensions will not fit. We do a final
measure of the space before anything is ordered, specifically to catch this.

## Beyond the kitchen

The same thinking applies to the other rooms these houses never planned for. We
regularly build mudrooms into back entries, home offices into spare bedrooms,
laundry areas into basements, and wet bars into finished basement spaces — all
places where an efficient layout and real storage change how the house works,
without touching a single exterior wall.
