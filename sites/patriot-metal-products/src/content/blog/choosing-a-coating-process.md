Most of the calls we get open the same way: "What would you charge to coat these?" It is a fair
question, and it is almost always the second question. The first one is which process the part
should run through at all — because the wrong process applied perfectly still fails in the field.

Four things decide it.

## 1. Geometry — can the process reach every surface?

A flat plate is easy. Almost anything will coat a flat plate. The trouble starts with tubular
structures, deep recesses, blind holes and the interior faces of welded assemblies.

Line-of-sight processes spray from the outside. Wherever the gun cannot see, the film thins out or
stops, and that is exactly where corrosion starts. If your part has interior volume that matters,
**autophoretic coating** is usually the answer. A-coat is waterborne and deposits through a chemical
reaction with the ferrous surface, so the rule of thumb is *where it wets, it coats* — inside,
outside and edges, at uniform thickness, with no drips, runs or sags. It will coat assembled
ferrous parts and sub-assemblies without coating the non-ferrous components.

## 2. Substrate — what is the part actually made of?

Hot rolled steel, cold rolled steel, cast iron and zinc diecast all behave differently in a coating
line. Autophoretic depends on a reaction with ferrous surfaces, which is a strength on steel
assemblies and a hard limit on anything non-ferrous. Powder is far less fussy about substrate but
much fussier about how clean the surface is when it arrives at the gun.

This is the point where prep stops being a detail. Nearly every coating failure we are asked to
diagnose — chipping, flaking, early rust creep — traces back to what happened before the coating,
not to the coating itself. Our powder line runs behind a six-stage iron phosphate washer for exactly
that reason, and we do our own burn-off, grinding, sanding, wheel abrading and blasting in-house
rather than depending on a part arriving clean.

## 3. Volume — one piece, or a truckload?

Volume changes the economics more than anything else on this list.

- **High production, small parts.** The powder line is built for it: sixteen robotic spray guns,
  components up to 22 feet long by 5 feet high by 47 inches wide, up to 2,000 pounds.
- **Fasteners and small hardware in bulk.** Barrel zinc plating. For longer or appearance-sensitive
  parts, rack plating handles up to 10 feet.
- **Large quantities needing an economical primer.** Dip coating, submerged, at an average film
  build of 1 to 2 dry mils, air cured.
- **One-offs and oversized items.** The batch booth. A single motorcycle frame and a 15-foot tank
  are both normal work in a room-sized booth and oven measuring 15' w x 20' l x 6' h.

We process orders ranging from one piece to entire truckloads daily, so volume should shape the
process choice — it should not decide whether you can get the job done at all.

## 4. Service environment — what will the part live through?

Indoor, outdoor, humid, salted, chemically exposed, high heat, UV, buried. Each one changes the
answer. Autophoretic delivers good cyclic corrosion protection, heat stability and impact
resistance, which is why it ends up on vehicle frames and chassis components. Zinc gives corrosion
resistance with a cosmetically pleasing finish and is well suited to hardware. Powder gives
outstanding corrosion resistance plus a durable decorative finish, and is available in primers,
topcoat, zinc rich, and high or low temperature formulations.

For government and defense work, CARC finishes — high temperature and high chemical resistance —
meeting MIL, OMAH and ANSI specifications run through our batch process.

## The part most shops skip: proving it

Picking the right process is half the job. Being able to show that the process ran correctly is the
other half, and it is where a lot of specifications stall.

All of our processes are supported by a state-of-the-art onsite coating laboratory. We provide
certifications of the processes and the performance criteria for varied customers and applications.
If your customer's spec has to be closed out on paper, you get paper.

## What to send us

Do not send a part number on its own — it tells us nothing we can quote against. Send:

- A drawing or photo of the part, including interior geometry if it has any
- The substrate
- Annual or per-run volume
- The service environment
- The finish spec or performance target you have to meet

We will tell you which of our five processes fits, or tell you honestly if none of them do. Both
answers are more useful than a guess.
