Every January the same call comes in. The roof was fine all summer, fine through the autumn rain, and then one week in February there is a brown ring spreading across a bedroom ceiling. Homeowners assume the roof failed. Usually it did not.

## What is actually happening

Heat escapes from the living space into the attic. That heat warms the underside of the roof deck across the middle of the roof — the part sitting over conditioned space — and the snow lying on it melts. The meltwater runs down the slope until it reaches the overhang, which hangs out past the wall over cold outside air and is therefore still below freezing. There it refreezes.

Do that for a few days and you build a ridge of ice at the eave. Now the meltwater coming down behind it has nowhere to go, so it pools. Water sitting still against a shingle roof will find its way under the shingles, because shingles are designed to shed water running downhill, not to hold back a standing pond. Once it is under the shingles it is inside the house.

That is an ice dam. Notice how much of that description is about attic heat and how little of it is about the roof.

## Why it is not really a roofing problem

Two houses on the same street, same age, same roof, and one dams every winter while the other never has. The difference is almost always what is between the ceiling and the roof deck: how much insulation, and how well the attic is ventilated. Insulation keeps the heat downstairs where you are paying for it. Ventilation flushes whatever heat does get up there before it can warm the deck. Get both right and the snow on the roof stays frozen from ridge to eave, and there is nothing to run down and refreeze.

Recessed lights, bath fans venting into the attic instead of outside, and gaps around the attic hatch are the usual culprits. So is insulation that has been compressed or pushed aside over the years.

## What roofing can do about it

One thing, and it matters: ice-and-water membrane at the eaves.

This is a self-adhering rubberised sheet that goes down on the deck before the underlayment and seals around fasteners. It runs from the edge of the roof up past the line of the exterior wall, which is to say past the point where an ice dam can form. If water does back up behind a dam, it hits a continuous waterproof layer rather than the seam between two shingles.

It does not stop the dam forming. It stops the dam getting into your house. On a re-roof we install it at every eave and through every valley as standard, not as an upgrade line item, because in this climate a roof without it is a roof waiting for the wrong February.

## If you already have water coming in

Do not go up there with a hammer. Chipping at an ice dam breaks shingles, and people fall off roofs doing it every winter. Raking snow off the lower few feet of the roof from the ground is safe and does help. Beyond that, get someone out to look.
