Every year the same thing happens. A system forms in the Atlantic, the cone shifts toward South Florida, and every tree company in Miami-Dade stops taking new work because they are committed for the next fortnight.

By then it is too late to do anything useful about the oak leaning over your roof.

## The window is spring, not September

Hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November. Miami-Dade County's own guidance is to prune ahead of the season rather than during it, and to use someone qualified.

That puts the useful window in late winter and spring — crews have availability, the ground is dry enough to get equipment onto a lawn without wrecking it, and the tree has months to respond to the cuts before it is asked to stand up to 90 mph.

Pruning in the 48 hours before landfall does not make a tree safer. It makes it a tree with fresh wounds and a pile of cut debris on the ground, which is now airborne debris.

## What actually fails

Three things, in our experience, and none of them are the trees that look unhealthy.

**Deadwood.** Dead limbs are already detached in every way that matters. Wind finds them first, and they are the cheapest thing on this list to deal with.

**An unthinned canopy.** This is the big one. A dense canopy that has never been opened up behaves like a sail — it catches wind across its whole surface and transfers the load into the major unions and the root plate. Either a limb tears out or the whole tree goes over. The trees we cut up after Irma were overwhelmingly the big, healthy, magnificent ones nobody had touched.

**Limbs grown out over the roof.** These are the expensive failures. The limb does not have to break to cost you a roof; it only has to flex far enough to hit tile or fascia repeatedly for six hours.

All three are routine to deal with in April. None of them are fixable the week a storm is named.

## Why a "hurricane cut" is not storm prep

If a crew offers to take a third off the top of your tree cheaply and quickly, what they are selling is topping, and it is worse than doing nothing.

Topping cuts limbs back to stubs with no regard for where the next branch is. The tree responds with a burst of fast, weakly attached shoots around each wound. Two or three seasons later you have a canopy that is denser than before, attached far more weakly, and considerably more likely to shed a limb in a storm — plus decay that entered through the open stubs and is not reversible.

Miami-Dade County's guidance specifically tells homeowners not to allow topping. It is the most damaging thing done to trees in this county, and it is done in the name of storm safety.

The same logic applies to palms. Stripping a palm back to a handful of upright fronds — the "hurricane cut" you see all over South Florida — does not protect it. The fronds are how the palm feeds itself and how it buffers wind at the crown. Take too many and you get a weaker tree with a thinner trunk laid down for the next several years.

## What correct storm prep looks like

**Deadwood out**, cut back to the branch collar so the tree can seal.

**Selective canopy thinning** — 10 to 20 percent of the interior growth, taken evenly throughout so wind passes through. Not hollowed out at the middle, which is its own bad practice and makes limb failure more likely.

**End weight reduced** on long lateral limbs, cut back to a lateral big enough to take over.

**Clearance established** over the roof, the driveway, the pool cage and the service drop.

**Palms cleared** of dead fronds and seed pods, live crown untouched.

That is ISA-standard work. It takes longer than tipping the ends off, and it is the difference between a tree that rides out a storm and one that costs you a roof.

## An inspection is worth more than a guess

The genuinely dangerous trees are usually not the ones you would pick from the driveway.

After Irma we spent weeks on properties across Cutler Bay, Palmetto Bay and Kendall cutting up trees that had looked completely healthy from the ground. What they had in common was internal damage from previous storms — cracked unions closed over by regrowth, hidden stub breaks, and root plates worked loose in saturated soil and never properly re-anchored.

You cannot see any of that from thirty feet below it.
