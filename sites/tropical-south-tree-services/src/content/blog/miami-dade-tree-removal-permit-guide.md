The short answer: probably, and the fines for guessing wrong are worse than the permit.

The longer answer depends on three things — what species the tree is, how big it is, and which municipality you are standing in. That last one catches people out constantly, because Miami-Dade is not one jurisdiction for this purpose. It is a county ordinance with several cities running their own on top.

## The county layer: Miami-Dade DERM

Miami-Dade County's Department of Environmental Resources Management administers the countywide tree ordinance, and it applies everywhere in the county including all the unincorporated communities.

Under it, **protected and heritage species require a permit before removal**. The protected list covers a lot of what is actually growing in Miami-Dade gardens — live oak, gumbo limbo, mahogany and several palms among them — and "heritage" generally captures specimens above a certain size regardless of species.

Removal without approval is an enforcement matter, and replacement planting is commonly required as a condition of approval. On a large protected specimen the county can require several replacement trees for the one removed.

## The city layer: where it gets more complicated

If your property is in an unincorporated community — Kendall, Perrine, The Hammocks, Country Walk, Palmetto Estates, Richmond West, Kendall West, Goulds, Redland — the county is the only authority. One process, one set of rules. That is the straightforward case.

If you are in one of the incorporated municipalities, you may have two.

**Coral Gables** administers its own tree removal permits in coordination with DERM. Protected species including live oak, banyan, gumbo limbo and mahogany require a **city** permit before any work begins. Replacement requirements are enforced routinely and fines are substantial. The City Beautiful takes its canopy seriously — it was planned around it in 1925.

**Pinecrest** enforces its ordinance rigorously through the Village. Protected species need a village permit before removal begins, replacement trees are commonly required, and unpermitted removal can trigger significant fines plus mandatory replanting, sometimes at several trees for each one taken.

**Homestead** applies additional urban forestry considerations for trees in the public right-of-way and city green space, alongside the county rules for private property.

**City of Miami** has its own urban forestry regulations for right-of-way and city-owned land; on private property, DERM rules govern.

**Redland and agricultural parcels** are a case of their own. On agriculturally classified land, grove and specimen trees — generally those at 18 inches trunk diameter or above — can fall under different provisions than a standard residential removal. Classification is the deciding factor, and it is not always obvious from the deed.

## Then there is your HOA

Entirely separate from any of the above.

Plenty of communities in Kendall, South Miami, Country Walk, The Hammocks, Richmond West and Kendall West have governing documents requiring **board approval before removal or major trimming**, including on private property. South Miami in particular has a very active HOA landscape off Red Road and along SW 57th Avenue.

County approval does not satisfy your association, and association approval does not satisfy the county. You need both, and the most common expensive mistake we see is someone who obtained one and assumed it covered the other.

## What the process actually involves

**Identify the tree.** Species and trunk diameter at breast height decide whether it is protected. This is worth getting right rather than guessing from a photo.

**Determine your authority.** County only, or county plus city. See above.

**Application.** Species, size, condition, location on the plot, and the reason for removal. Hazard and disease are the strongest grounds; "it drops leaves in my pool" generally is not.

**Supporting documentation.** For a protected specimen, the application will frequently need a written assessment from a qualified arborist — particularly where the argument is that the tree is hazardous. This is why the ISA credential matters: the people reading these applications check it.

**Replacement conditions.** Approval usually comes with a requirement to plant something back, sometimes more than one, sometimes of a specified species and size.

## What it costs to skip it

Fines, mandatory replacement planting at the county's chosen ratio, and a stop-work order if you are caught mid-job.

There is also a resale dimension people forget. Unpermitted removal of a protected tree is the kind of thing that surfaces during a property transaction and becomes the buyer's problem to raise and yours to resolve, at exactly the moment you have least leverage.
