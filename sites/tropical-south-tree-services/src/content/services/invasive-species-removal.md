## What we remove

**Brazilian pepper** — the most widespread invasive in South Florida. It forms dense thickets, shades out everything native, and resprouts vigorously from any stump left in the ground.

**Melaleuca** — the paperbark planted decades ago to dry out the Everglades, now one of the worst invasives in the state. Enormous seed loads, and cutting one without care triggers a mass seed release.

**Earleaf acacia** — fast, brittle, and a reliable source of storm debris. Shallow-rooted and prone to going over in wind.

**Australian pine** — shallow-rooted, salt-tolerant, and notorious for toppling in hurricanes. It also acidifies the soil beneath it so thoroughly that little else will grow.

## Why they keep coming back

Because they are cut rather than removed.

Most of these species resprout from the stump, and several regenerate from root fragments left in the soil. Cut a Brazilian pepper at the base and you get a multi-stemmed thicket where you had one trunk.

Removing them means taking out the root system, grinding the stump below grade, and clearing the seedlings that have already established in the surrounding soil. Half a job here is genuinely worse than none — you end up with a denser, more difficult problem than you started with.

## Timing melaleuca

Melaleuca releases its seed when the tree is stressed or cut. Removing one carelessly can scatter an enormous seed load across ground you have just cleared, and you will be pulling seedlings for years.

It needs handling deliberately, and it is a good reason not to take one on yourself with a chainsaw at the weekend.

## After removal

Cleared ground in South Florida does not stay empty. Something is going to colonise it, and if you do not choose what, the invasives will choose for you.

Replanting with appropriate natives is the durable fix, and it is worth planning at the same time as the removal rather than a year later.
