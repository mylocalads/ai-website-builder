## What correct mulching looks like

**Two to four inches deep.** Deeper than that and water and air stop reaching the roots.

**Out to the drip line where you can.** The feeder roots that actually matter are out at the edge of the canopy, not against the trunk.

**A clear gap at the trunk.** Several inches of bare soil all the way round. The root flare should be visible — if you cannot see where the trunk widens into the roots, the mulch is too deep or too close.

**Topped up, not stacked.** Refresh annually as it breaks down rather than laying new mulch over old until the depth doubles.

## The mulch volcano

The cone of mulch heaped up the trunk is the most common tree-care mistake in South Florida, and it is genuinely damaging.

Bark held permanently wet begins to rot, which lets in decay and insects at exactly the point the tree can least afford it. Roots respond by growing up into the mulch and circling the trunk, and years later those girdling roots strangle the tree. Rodents nest in it and chew the bark over winter.

The tree does not die quickly, which is the problem — by the time it declines visibly, the damage has been accumulating for years and nobody links it back to the mulch.

If your trees have volcanoes around them, pulling the mulch back off the flare is free and worth doing this weekend.

## Why it matters here

Through the dry season a mulched root zone holds water dramatically better than bare soil under Miami sun. And in the wet season, mulch protects the thin topsoil layer over the county's limestone from simply washing off in a downpour.
