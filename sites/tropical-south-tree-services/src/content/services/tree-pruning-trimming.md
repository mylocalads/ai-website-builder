## Why pruning matters more here

South Florida does not give trees an off-season. They grow twelve months a year, which means a canopy that was fine last spring is into the roofline by this one, and a limb that was clear of the power drop has closed the gap.

Then there is June through November. The trees that fail in a storm are almost always carrying one of three things: deadwood, an unthinned canopy that catches wind like a sail, or a heavy limb grown out over the roof. All three are routine to fix in the spring. None of them are fixable the week a storm is named.

## What we prune

**Structural pruning** — establishing and maintaining a sound branch architecture, so the tree carries its own weight properly as it matures.

**Palm trimming** — dead fronds and seed pods removed without cutting into the live crown. Over-pruning a palm into a narrow "hurricane cut" weakens it; the fronds are how it feeds itself and how it buffers wind at the crown.

**Storm-prep thinning** — selective removal through the canopy to reduce wind resistance while keeping the tree's natural shape and size.

**Deadwood removal** — dead limbs are already detached in every way that matters. Wind finds them first.

**Roofline and utility clearance** — limbs lifted off the roof, the gutters, the pool cage and the drop line.

## We do not hatrack

Topping a tree — cutting the main limbs back to stubs — is the fastest way to make a canopy smaller and the most reliable way to ruin the tree permanently. Miami-Dade County's own guidance tells homeowners not to allow it.

What grows back after topping is a cluster of weak shoots attached to the outside of the old wound rather than properly joined to the limb. Two or three seasons on you have a tree that is denser than it was, structurally weaker where it counts, and more likely to shed a limb than if it had been left alone.

If a crew quotes you a cheap "hurricane cut" that involves taking a third off the top, that is what they mean.
