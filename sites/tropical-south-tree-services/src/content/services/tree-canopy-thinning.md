## Why thinning is the best storm-prep job there is

The trees that fail in a Miami-Dade hurricane are rarely the weak-looking ones. They are the big, dense, healthy-looking specimens whose canopies have never been opened up.

A solid canopy catches wind across its whole surface. The load has to go somewhere, and it goes into the major unions and the root plate. Thin that canopy and the wind passes through instead of pushing, and the same tree in the same storm sees a fraction of the force.

It is also the job that best preserves what you actually like about a mature tree. Height stays. Spread stays. Shade stays. What leaves is the congestion inside.

## What comes out

**Interior deadwood** — the dead limbs inside the canopy that wind finds first.

**Crossing and rubbing limbs** — branches abrading each other create wounds and entry points for decay.

**Congested growth** — thinning evenly throughout so the canopy keeps a natural density rather than being hollowed out at the middle, which is a real risk when it is done badly.

**Weak, poorly attached branches** — particularly narrow-angled unions carrying included bark.

## Thinning versus reduction

They solve different problems and are often confused.

**Thinning** keeps the tree the same size and reduces the density inside it. It is for wind load and light.

**Reduction** makes the tree smaller. It is for a tree that has outgrown its space.

Plenty of trees need both, particularly a mature live oak or ficus close to a house. We will tell you which yours needs, and if the honest answer is that it needs neither this year, we will tell you that too.
