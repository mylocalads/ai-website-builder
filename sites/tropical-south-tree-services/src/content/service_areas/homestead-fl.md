## No community was shaped by a storm more

Hurricane Andrew made landfall directly on Homestead on 24 August 1992 with winds over 165 mph — the most destructive hurricane to hit the continental United States at that point.

Everything about tree work in this city traces back to that day. The canopy that exists now is largely what was planted afterwards, which means a generation of trees that are now big enough to matter in a storm, on a stock of housing much of which was also rebuilt post-Andrew.

Homestead is also the part of Miami-Dade a storm coming up from the Keys or in off the Atlantic reaches first. That is not an abstraction to people who live here.

## Three different jobs in one city

**Newer master-planned communities** — Waterstone, Keys Gate, Baywinds. Development-installed landscaping, trees now reaching the size where structural pruning determines what they become. Getting this right now is what avoids removals in fifteen years.

**Older neighbourhoods near the centre.** Mature live oak, mahogany and large ficus, often close to houses. Structural work, deadwood and canopy thinning.

**Agricultural land at the edges.** Groves, long fence lines, acreage. A different scale entirely — more chipping capacity, more haul-off, and full days rather than half-hour visits.

## We keep our own storm equipment

Our chipper and chip truck are ours, not hired when a storm is forecast. In south Dade that distinction is the whole thing: when every crew in the county is trying to rent equipment at once, there is none to rent, and debris that cannot be chipped sits at the kerb for weeks.

## Permits in Homestead

Removals fall under **Miami-Dade County DERM** for protected and heritage species. The City of Homestead additionally applies urban forestry considerations to trees in the public right-of-way and in city-managed green space.

Agricultural parcels can follow different rules again depending on classification. We work out which applies to your property before quoting.
