Every winter we get the same call, usually in late January or February. There is a
brown stain spreading on a bedroom ceiling. It was not there in the fall. The roof is
not that old. And when the homeowner goes outside and looks up, nothing appears to be
wrong.

That is an ice dam, and it is the single most common cause of winter roof leaks in
Northeast Pennsylvania.

## How an ice dam forms

Start with snow sitting on your roof. Warm air from the house collects in the attic and
warms the underside of the roof deck. The snow directly above that warm deck melts —
even on a day when it never gets above freezing outside.

That meltwater runs downhill under the remaining snow until it reaches the eave: the
overhang past the exterior wall, which has no warm attic beneath it and is therefore
still at outdoor temperature. There the water refreezes.

Do that for a few days and you build a ridge of solid ice along the bottom edge of the
roof. Now the next round of meltwater arrives and cannot get past it. It pools behind
the dam and sits there.

This is the part that matters: **asphalt shingles are not waterproof.** They are
designed to shed water downhill, fast. They rely on gravity and overlap. Standing water
behind an ice dam works sideways and upward under the shingle courses, past the
overlap, and onto the decking. From there it travels along rafters — often several feet
— before it finds a spot to drip through insulation and drywall.

Which is why the stain on your ceiling is rarely directly under the leak, and why the
problem has usually been running for weeks by the time you see anything.

## Why this is a ventilation and drainage problem

The instinct is to treat it as a roof-covering problem. It usually is not.

**Attic ventilation.** The melting starts because warm air is reaching the underside of
the deck. Soffit vents let cold outside air in at the eaves and ridge vents let warm air
out at the top, keeping the deck close to outdoor temperature so the snow on top of it
stays frozen. When soffit vents are blocked — by insulation pushed into them, by damaged
or painted-over soffit panels, by a fascia repair that closed them off — the deck warms
and the cycle starts. Insufficient attic insulation contributes for the same reason.

**Drainage.** Gutters do not cause ice dams, but clogged, undersized or badly pitched
gutters make them considerably worse by holding water and ice right at the eave where the
dam wants to form. Gutters full of last autumn's leaves freeze into a solid block early
and stay that way all season.

This is why we treat gutters, soffit and fascia as part of the roof system rather than
as trim. Replacing shingles on a roof with a ventilation problem gets you a new roof with
the same leak.

## What actually helps

- **Ice-and-water shield at the eaves and valleys.** A self-adhering membrane installed
  under the shingles along the bottom courses and in every valley. It seals around
  fastener penetrations and gives you a waterproof layer exactly where standing water
  occurs. This goes on as standard during any replacement we do.
- **Clear, correctly sized, correctly pitched gutters.** Cleaned before winter, with leaf
  guards where tree cover justifies them, and enough downspouts for the roof area actually
  draining into them.
- **Open soffit venting and adequate attic insulation.** Keep the deck cold and the melt
  never starts.
- **Getting the snow off the lower roof.** A roof rake used from the ground on the first
  three or four feet above the eave removes the source of the meltwater. Do not go up on a
  snow-covered roof to do this.

## What not to do

Do not chip at the ice with a hammer or a shovel. We have replaced a lot of shingles that
survived the ice and did not survive the removal. Do not run salt in a sock across the
roof as a permanent fix — it can help thaw a channel in an emergency, but it corrodes
flashing and fasteners and it does nothing about the cause.
