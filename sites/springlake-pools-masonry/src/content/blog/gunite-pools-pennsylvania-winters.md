Most pool advice on the internet is written for places that do not freeze. It is genuinely useful if your pool is in Phoenix. It is close to worthless in Dallas, Pennsylvania, where the same pool has to survive 90°F in July and -10°F in January, sometimes crossing the freezing line and back again several times in a single week.

That crossing is the whole problem. It has a name — the freeze-thaw cycle — and it is the single largest factor in whether a pool here is still sound in twenty years or has been a running repair bill since year eight.

## What freeze-thaw actually does

Water expands when it freezes, by roughly nine percent. That is a small number until you consider where the water is.

Water gets into hairline cracks in plaster. It gets into the joints under coping stones. It gets into the ground behind the shell and into any plumbing that was not properly drained in the fall. When it freezes it pushes outward with a force that concrete simply was not asked to resist, and when it thaws it leaves the gap slightly wider than it found it. Then it happens again.

That is why the failures here are so predictable. Cracked and rough plaster after ten to fifteen years. Coping that has lifted just enough to let water get behind the shell. Deck slabs that heave. Plumbing that split over the winter and only announces itself when the pool is filled in April.

## Why the shell material matters

Gunite is concrete sprayed at high velocity against a steel reinforcing cage rather than poured into a form. The velocity is the point: it compacts the material as it lands, producing a dense, monolithic shell with no seams and no panels.

Two things follow from that. The first is strength — it is the strongest shell type available, and gunite pools built properly around here are still sound after more than twenty winters. The second is shape. Because the shell is formed rather than delivered, the pool can be whatever the lot allows. A narrow rectangle where a valley lot leaves no room. A curve around a mature oak. A shell that steps down a grade instead of demanding the grade be removed.

That flexibility is not a luxury feature in this region. It is what lets a pool sit on a hillside lot in Dallas Township or on the rocky ledge that shows up near Bear Creek without an excavation budget that swallows the project.

## The part that happens before anyone pours anything

The most consequential decisions on a pool build happen before the shell exists, during excavation.

The ground is not consistent across a twenty-mile radius here. There is rocky terrain near Harvey's Lake. There are clay deposits through the Back Mountain that hold water and move with the seasons. There is flatter, better-behaved ground down near the university. A dig plan that ignores which of those it is standing on is the origin of most of the problems that show up later.

Plumbing depth is the other one. Lines have to run below frost level, full stop. It is not visible in a finished pool, no homeowner ever inspects it, and it is one of the few mistakes that cannot be corrected without demolition.

## What you can control after the pool is built

Even a well-built shell needs two things every year.

**A real closing.** Winterization means drawing water down, blowing out and draining the lines below frost level, adding antifreeze where the layout calls for it, and getting a proper cover on. A closing done quickly and a closing done correctly look identical in November. They look very different in April.

**Attention to the surface.** Plaster and waterline tile are the wear layer, and they are supposed to be replaced eventually — that is not a defect. Resurfacing at the ten-to-fifteen-year mark restores the finish and closes the small leaks that freeze-thaw has been widening. Doing it on schedule is dramatically cheaper than doing it after water has been getting behind the shell for three years.

## What to ask a builder here

If you are interviewing pool builders in Northeastern Pennsylvania, three questions separate the ones who work in this climate from the ones who work in a catalogue:

1. What did you find when you looked at my soil, and how does the excavation plan change because of it?
2. How deep are the lines going, and what does your winterization actually include?
3. Who does the coping and the deck — your people or a subcontractor you will be scheduling around?

The third one matters more than it sounds. Masonry is where water gets behind a shell, and hardscape fitted around someone else's finished work is never as tight as hardscape built as part of the same job.
