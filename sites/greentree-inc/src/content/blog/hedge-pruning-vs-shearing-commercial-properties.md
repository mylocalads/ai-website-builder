A hedge is cut, it looks sharp for a fortnight, and then it does not. The outer
surface goes shaggy, the base thins out, and by the next scheduled visit the whole
thing needs doing again. This is usually not neglect. It is the predictable result
of shearing a plant that needed pruning.

## What shearing actually does

Shearing cuts everything at a uniform plane, regardless of where the cut falls on
each stem. It is fast, it looks immediately tidy, and it triggers a specific
response from the plant: dense new shoots at every cut tip.

That dense outer shell is the problem. It shades the interior, the inner foliage
dies back, and the hedge becomes a thin green skin over bare wood. Once that has
happened, the plant cannot be cut back into the bare interior without leaving a hole
that may take a season or more to fill — so each cut has to stay slightly outside
the last one, and the hedge slowly grows out of its allotted space year after year.

## What pruning does instead

Pruning cuts to the plant's structure: back to a lateral branch or an outward-facing
bud, so growth is directed rather than simply stimulated. It takes longer, it looks
less immediately geometric on the day, and it holds its shape considerably longer.
More importantly, it keeps light reaching the interior, so the plant retains foliage
through its depth and can be cut back when it eventually needs to be.

On a commercial property, this is a budget question as much as a horticultural one.
Shearing needs doing more often, degrades the plant material, and eventually forces
a hard renovation cut or a replacement planting that nobody budgeted for.

## What to look for on your own property

Push a hand into the hedge past the outer surface. If you find bare woody stems
within a few inches, it is being sheared. If you find foliage most of the way in, it
is being pruned.

Then look at the shape. A hedge maintained correctly is very slightly wider at the
base than at the top, so the lower foliage still receives light. A hedge that is
vertical-sided or wider at the top is guaranteed to thin out at the bottom — and the
bottom is the part at eye level for everyone walking past it.

## Why cycle matters more than technique

Even correct pruning fails if it happens on complaint rather than on schedule. South
Florida's growing season gives a hedge a great deal of time to get away from you, and
a plant allowed to overgrow can generally only be brought back with the kind of hard
cut that leaves it looking damaged for months.

The cheapest hedge on a property is the one that has never been allowed to get out
of shape.
