The most common complaint property managers have about landscape contractors is not
that the work was bad. It is that the work they thought they had bought was never
in the contract to begin with.

A low bid wins because it is low. It stops being low around month three, when the
change orders start arriving for things everybody assumed were included. By the end
of the first year the cheaper contractor has cost more than the one that quoted
honestly, and the property has spent twelve months looking worse.

Here is what a maintenance scope should say plainly.

## Frequency, in writing, per service

"Regular maintenance" is not a frequency. Mowing, edging, weeding, hedge pruning
and irrigation inspection all run on different cycles, and each one should have its
own stated interval. If the contract says only that the contractor will "maintain
the grounds", you have no basis to say anything has been missed.

Watch for frequency that changes with the season. South Florida's growing season is
long, and a schedule that is right in February is wrong in July. A contractor who
has thought about this will say so; one who has not will simply visit less often
when the property needs them more.

## Who does the work

Ask directly whether the crews are the contractor's own. A great many are not. The
signs are consistent: an unmarked truck, crew in a different company's shirts, and
nobody on site who knows the property's history. Subcontracting is not automatically
a problem, but discovering it after signing always is — and it usually means the
company you vetted is not the company doing the work.

## What counts as an extra

Storm debris. Seasonal colour changes. Irrigation repairs. Plant replacement.
Palm frond removal. Each of these is legitimately quotable as an extra, and each is
routinely assumed to be included. Get the list of what is *not* covered, in writing,
before you sign. A contractor who is comfortable giving you that list is telling you
something about how they intend to invoice.

## Reporting

The single most valuable line in a maintenance contract is the one that says the
contractor will tell you about problems. A failing irrigation zone found during a
scheduled inspection is a repair. The same zone found after the turf dies is a
replanting. The difference is entirely whether somebody was looking, and whether
they told you.

## The question that sorts contractors quickly

Ask what happens in month thirty-six.

Almost every commercial landscape contractor performs well in the first ninety days.
The complaint you will hear from other property managers is not about the start of
the relationship — it is that the edges got sloppy, the calls stopped being returned,
and the invoice never changed. Ask for properties the contractor has held for a
decade, and call them. That conversation tells you more than the proposal does.
