Brown patches on a commercial lawn generate the same email every time: the board or
the tenant has noticed, and the property manager needs an answer. The difficulty is
that four completely different problems produce a nearly identical symptom, and
treating the wrong one costs a season.

## Mowing height

This is the most common cause and the easiest to miss, because nothing about it
looks like a mistake. St. Augustine — the dominant turf on South Florida commercial
properties — is cut too short far more often than it is cut too long. Scalping
removes the leaf area the plant photosynthesises with, stresses the root system, and
opens the canopy to weeds. The lawn browns, and the obvious conclusion is that it
needs water or chemicals, when what it needed was to be left half an inch longer.

A contractor running one mower height across every property in a county will produce
this on some of them. It is worth asking what height your turf is being cut at, and
why.

## Chinch bugs

Chinch bug pressure in South Florida is effectively year-round, and St. Augustine is
their preferred host. The damage pattern is characteristic: irregular patches in the
sunniest, driest part of the lawn — often along pavements and parking edges where
heat is reflected — that expand outward and do not recover with watering.

The test is simple and worth doing before anybody applies anything. Push apart the
turf at the edge of a browning patch, in the green tissue rather than the dead
centre, and look for small dark insects moving at the soil line.

## Thatch

Thatch builds fast in a long growing season. Past about half an inch it starts
behaving like a sponge that holds water at the surface and keeps it from the roots,
so the lawn is simultaneously overwatered and drought-stressed. It also makes
insecticide and fertiliser applications far less effective, which is why a property
with a thatch problem often appears not to respond to treatment.

## Irrigation

The one that is most often blamed on the landscaper and least often caused by them.
A broken head, an arc rotated to point at a pavement, or a zone that has silently
stopped running produces dry patches with hard geometric edges — a straight line or
a fan shape, rather than the irregular spread of a pest problem.

This is the strongest argument for inspecting irrigation on a schedule instead of
after a complaint. The pattern of the brown patch tells you which problem you have,
and a contractor who checks coverage first will not spend your budget treating for
insects that were never there.

## The order to work through it

Look at the shape of the patch. Geometric edges point at irrigation. Irregular
spread in the hottest part of the lawn points at chinch bugs. Uniform thinning
across the whole property points at mowing height or thatch.

Diagnose, then treat. That is the entire principle behind Integrated Pest
Management, and it is the difference between fixing a lawn and spending a summer
applying things to it.
